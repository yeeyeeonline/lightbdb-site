What does "hand finishing" actually include on a wood branch sculpture lamp?

Hand finishing covers every step after the branch body leaves the lathe or CNC: sanding, knot filling, vine and armature wrapping, stain or patina, wire routing and a final light-on check.

The machining of the branch — turning, tapering, drilling the wire channel — is where most buyers think the cost sits, because it looks dramatic. In practice, machining is fast and repeatable. The expensive half is the bench work that turns a machined blank into something a customer wants to touch.

The six bench operations that make up the premium

  1. Progressive hand sanding. Three grits is standard, four on visible crotches and knots. Most factories in this category spend 25–60 minutes per lamp here.
  2. Knot filling and grain repair. Epoxy or wood filler into voids, then re-sanded flush. Five to fifteen minutes per piece, more on low-grade stock.
  3. Vine, leaf and armature wrapping. Hand-wrapped detail, or hand-concealing the metal armature inside the branch. This is usually the single largest line item and the hardest to automate.
  4. Colour work. A wash, stain, glaze or multi-layer patina, hand-applied so the grain reads. Eight to twenty-five minutes of application, plus drying that you cannot rush.
  5. Wire routing and final assembly. Feeding cable through the branch channel, fitting the socket, felt-padding the base, hand-tightening. Ten to twenty minutes.
  6. Light-on test and touch-up. A 100% function test, then colour-matched touch-up on any scratch found at inspection.

How much does hand finishing add to the unit cost of a wood branch lamp?

Industry-typical hand finishing adds US$14–42 per unit at a 300-piece run, which is 15–35% of the FOB unit price for a mid-size branch lamp.

That spread is wide because two lamps in the same catalogue can differ by an hour of bench time. A single-stem table lamp with a matte oil finish sits near the bottom; a four-branch floor sculpture with wrapped vines, a two-tone patina and a fabric shade sits near the top.

One sanity check you can run on any quote: loaded finishing labour in Shenzhen typically lands between US$6 and US$12 per hour. A 45-minute finish is therefore roughly US$4.50–9 of direct labour before overhead, consumables, rework and yield loss. If a supplier quotes US$3 for a finish you can see took 45 minutes, the difference will show up somewhere else — thinner sanding, fewer grits, or a rework rate they have not priced in.

Which design details drive labour minutes up fastest?

Branch count, joint count and finish complexity drive labour minutes far more than wood species does.

Ranked by cost impact

  • Number of branches. Every extra branch adds sanding and wrapping time almost linearly. Going from one stem to four often adds 30–50 minutes.
  • Visible joints and grafts. Each joint needs blending and colour matching. Five joints can add more time than a whole additional stem.
  • Wrapped or sculpted detail. Hand-wrapped vines, leaves or twine are the least automatable operation in the build.
  • Multi-layer finishes. A single matte oil is one pass. A two-tone patina with a rubbed-back highlight is three passes plus drying between each.
  • Shade and diffuser complexity. Fabric shades with hand-fitted spiders, or glass diffusers hand-set into a branch cup, add hand-assembly time that buyers rarely budget for.
  • Wood species. Real but smaller. Open-grain species such as oak and ash need more filling than tight-grain species, but the gap is usually 10–20%, not 100%.

Those tiers exist because hand finishing has a fixed setup cost that has to be absorbed somewhere: building the finishing jig, matching the colour to your reference, and running first-article approval with your team. At 100 pieces that setup is spread thin; at 1,000 it is negligible.

Below roughly 100 pieces, per-unit finishing cost rises steeply across the category — most factories in this category will either decline or quote a sampling-rate price. If you are testing a market, the practical route is a 100-piece standard run in an existing finish, then move to a custom finish once the SKU proves itself.

How long does hand finishing add to sampling and production?

Plan on 7–14 days for a sample and 4–8 weeks from deposit to FOB Shenzhen for production, with hand finishing inside that window rather than added to it.

The reason finishing does not simply stack onto lead time is that it runs in parallel cells — bodies are machined in batches, then released to the bench in waves. What does extend the calendar is drying. A multi-layer patina with proper flash-off between coats can add two to four days to a batch, and humidity control in the finishing room determines whether that holds.

For a first order, add buffer for first-article approval and any colour revisions. Buyers who compress that step usually pay for it in rework on the full run.

Where the time actually goes

  1. Sample build and finish matching: 7–14 days.
  2. Sample approval and any colour revision: your side, typically 3–7 days.
  3. Material procurement and body machining: front-loaded, runs in parallel with finishing setup.
  4. Bench finishing in waves: the main constraint on daily output.
  5. Light-on test, inspection, packing: 2–4 days at the end.

On capacity: LIGHT BDB runs a 3,000 sqm finished-product assembly base in Shenzhen and a Yangon overflow line rated at 5,000+ pcs per day. Overflow capacity helps with volume, but hand finishing is trained bench work — you cannot surge it the way you can surge SMT. That is worth knowing before you promise a retailer a large repeat order on a short calendar.

What should I verify in a factory that claims hand-finishing capability?

Ask for ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL registered factory status and Sony Green Partner — then ask for unedited video of the actual finishing bench.

Certificates tell you the management system exists. They do not tell you whether the person sanding your lamp knows what a visible crotch looks like at three grits. The video does.

A practical audit checklist

  • Ask to see the finishing bench, not the showroom. Unedited video, current date.
  • Request the first-article inspection report from a recent branch-lamp run, with colour readings.
  • Ask what the rework rate is on hand-finished lamps and how it is recorded.

What are the honest trade-offs of hand-finished wood branch lamps?

Hand finishing buys character, repairability and a premium shelf presence, but it costs 15–35% more, varies piece to piece, and adds drying days you cannot compress.

None of that is a defect. It is the price of the category. Buyers who go in expecting machined consistency from a hand-finished product end up in disputes with their supplier and their retailer at the same time.

The risks worth pricing in

  • Piece-to-piece variation. No two branches are identical, and no two hand-applied patinas are either. Write a "character tolerance" sheet into your spec so your QC team and your supplier agree on what variation is acceptable before the run starts.
  • Rework allowance. Hand finishing carries a touch-up rate that most factories in this category will not volunteer. Ask for it, and make sure it is inside the quoted price rather than invoiced later.
  • Colour drift between batches. Hand-mixed stains drift. Retain a signed colour standard and re-approve it on every repeat order.
  • Labour cost inflation. Finishing is the most wage-exposed part of the build. Multi-year price holds on heavily hand-finished SKUs are harder to sustain than on machined ones.
  • Wood as a natural material. Species availability, moisture movement and cracking risk all sit outside the finishing bench. Seasoning discipline matters more than any finishing trick.
  • Finishing is not electrical safety. A beautifully finished lamp still needs a properly specified driver, socket and cable. Do not let finish quality stand in for electrical review.
  • Slower scale-up. A trained finishing bench takes weeks to build, not days. Plan repeat orders with that in mind.

Where hand finishing earns its premium is in categories where the object is meant to be looked at and touched — a sculptural lamp in a bedroom, a spa reception, a boutique hotel room. Where it does not earn its premium is in high-volume utility lighting, where buyers want identical units and the lowest landed cost. Being clear about which one you are building saves everyone a difficult conversation.

Can a hand-applied finish match my Pantone reference?

It can get close on a signed physical standard, but hand-applied stain on natural wood will not match a printed Pantone chip exactly. Approve a physical finish standard rather than a paper swatch, and keep that signed standard on file for every repeat order.

Why do two hand-finished lamps look slightly different?

Because the wood grain differs and the colour is applied by hand, small variation is inherent to the category. The fix is a written character tolerance in your specification so both your team and the factory judge the same way.
