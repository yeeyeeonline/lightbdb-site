Why is auto-off behaviour a specification instead of just a feature?

It is a spec because it is measured, documented, and tested against safety standards, whereas a feature is only marketed.

A feature is what the customer reads on the box: "smart timer", "auto shut-off", "safe session". A spec is the technical value behind that promise — how many minutes, what tolerance, what power state afterwards, and what proof exists that it works every time. When you brief a factory, "auto-off" as a feature is a paragraph; as a spec it is a testable requirement with a pass/fail line.

The difference becomes visible on your first return. If a unit fails to shut down, a feature-based supplier treats it as a software quirk; a spec-based supplier treats it as a defect covered by the warranty. LIGHT BDB treats it that way because the component chain is engineered for it. The Panyu component plant, operated with exclusive strategic partner Fuli Electronics, builds auto-grade inductors, transformers, and wireless charging coils on site — the same class of timing components used in automotive applications. Timer accuracy for LIGHT BDB SKUs is set at component level, not with cheap RC circuits that drift.

That is also why the spec affects certification. LIGHT BDB is a UL registered factory, which means the timer and cut-off logic on shipped units are part of the audited process, not a marketing bullet point.

What exactly should you specify when you brief auto-off behaviour?

Specify four values: timeout duration, power-cut behaviour, user feedback cues, and the reset procedure.

Most brands stop at the timeout duration and assume the rest. The other three values decide whether the product feels safe, predictable, and cheap to service. In your ODM brief, write them out explicitly so there is no ambiguity at the engineering hand-off.

The four values that define your auto-off spec

  1. Timeout duration — in minutes and seconds, with an accepted tolerance. Industry-typical tolerance for this category is roughly ±10%, but the tighter you spec, the more control you hold over battery drain and user expectations.
  2. Power-cut behaviour — full power cut versus standby. A full cut protects the battery and the heating element; standby keeps LED indicators alive and drains a small current. State which one you want, and the standby current limit if applicable.
  3. User feedback cues — beep, LED flash, or vibration before shut-off. This is a small firmware cost that dramatically reduces user complaints about "it just turned off".
  4. Reset procedure — single button press, double press, or re-plug. A bad reset flow creates confusing support tickets, so define it in the brief.

If you do not specify these, the factory will pick defaults, and you will discover the choice only after your first batch of customer reviews. LIGHT BDB's design centres in Hangzhou and Hamburg translate these four values into firmware specs before tooling starts, which is why the 4-8 week lead time from deposit to FOB Shenzhen usually holds without surprise revisions.

How does timer behaviour differ across product categories?

Heated body-care products need short thermal cut-offs, while light and mood platforms need longer, dimmable sessions.

The physics is different. A heating element can raise surface temperature quickly, so the timer must protect skin contact. A light panel or mood lamp is a thermal nonevent, but it affects circadian rhythm and user routine, so the timer serves a scheduling purpose instead. This is a horizontal guide: the same principle applies whether you sell a heated eye massager, a red light therapy panel, or a bedside mood lamp.

What certifications and tests verify auto-off and timer specifications?

UL factory registration and a per-SKU documented test report are the practical proof of auto-off compliance.

LIGHT BDB holds UL registered factory status, ISO 9001 for quality management, ISO 14001 for environmental management, BSCI (amfori) for social audit, and Sony Green Partner status. For a wellness-device ODM, these are the consumer-safety baseline a retail buyer or marketplace will ask about.

The tests behind the spec are what actually protect you. Industry-typical verification for a timer spec includes a thermal soak test at maximum session length, a timeout drift test to confirm the ±10% window, and a power-cycling test to prove the reset procedure survives repeated use. These are standard practice for any factory in this category that takes the timer seriously; the difference is whether the supplier hands you the report without asking.

What are the honest trade-offs of strict auto-off and timer settings?

Strict auto-off reduces safety risk but can frustrate users who want longer sessions, so balance is a brand decision.

There is a real tension here. A tight auto-off window is safer and extends battery life, but it produces reviews like "it turned off in the middle of my nap". A loose or fully manual timer improves perceived control but increases thermal risk and support load when users forget to switch off.

Three honest trade-offs to weigh before you brief:

  • Custom firmware raises your MOQ floor. Standard behaviour costs nothing at the base tier; a unique timer curve pushes you into the ODM tier, which starts at 500-1,000 pcs for light platforms and 2,000-3,000 pcs for body care.
  • Over-engineering accuracy adds cost with little user benefit. Specifying a ±2% timer tolerance on a wellness lamp is wasted money; ±10% is already tighter than any buyer will feel.
  • Feedback cues solve more complaints than tighter timers. A beep or flash before shut-off usually reduces return reasons more than shortening the session itself.

The practical path is to start with the standard spec, validate it with samples, and tune the timeout after your first customer feedback. LIGHT BDB ships standard units fast, then adjusts the curve in later production runs once you have real data instead of assumptions.
