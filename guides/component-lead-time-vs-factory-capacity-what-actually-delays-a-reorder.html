What actually delays a reorder: component lead time or factory capacity?

Component lead time delays most reorders; factory capacity delays the ones placed at peak season without a forecast.

Those are two different failure modes, and they need two different fixes. Capacity is elastic. A factory can add a shift, run through a weekend, or push volume to an overflow line. Components are not elastic. If a specific power IC, connector or bin-matched LED is on allocation, no amount of line capacity converts into finished goods. You can have a completely empty assembly floor and still miss your ship date by five weeks.

Buyers misdiagnose this constantly. A PO slips two weeks and the instinct is to assume the factory is juggling too many clients. In our experience the more common cause is a part that was approved at sample stage, then re-quoted at mass-production stage and found to be on an eight-week lead. The factory has the hours. It does not have the parts.

Signals that your delay is component-side

  • Your confirmed ship date moves, but the factory's line schedule still shows your slot.
  • The delay appears at material release rather than at assembly.
  • Substitution requests start arriving — "can we use the 470µH instead of the 330µH?" That is a component problem, not a capacity problem.
  • Several factories you sampled all quote similar delays, which points to a market-wide part rather than one supplier's floor.

Signals that your delay is capacity-side

  • Only your assembly slot moves; material was received weeks ago.
  • The delay tracks seasonality — pre-Q4, pre-Lunar New Year, or the weeks after a major trade show.
  • Your order sat below the platform MOQ and had to be scheduled around larger runs.

Which components have the longest lead times in heated massagers and red light therapy devices?

The long-lead items are usually power ICs, connectors and specialty LEDs; magnetics are fast at LIGHT BDB because we wind them in Panyu.

This inverts what many first-time importers expect. The component that looks most custom — the wound inductor or the wireless charging coil — is often the one we control end to end. The component that looks like a commodity — a small power management IC — is the one that can quietly add two months.

Why magnetics are rarely your bottleneck

The Panyu plant is 10,000+ sqm with 600+ staff, operated by our exclusive strategic manufacturing partner Fuli Electronics. It produces auto-grade inductors, transformers, wireless charging coils and SMT in-house. On a reorder where the magnetics spec has not changed, that work starts immediately rather than waiting on an external winding house's queue. That single fact removes one of the most commonly quoted excuses in this product category.

Where the real wait sits

  • Power management ICs and MCUs. Industry-typical 8–16 weeks when a part is on allocation, and longer for a new part number entering a fab queue.
  • Specialty LEDs and diode arrays for red light products. Industry-typical 4–10 weeks, with bin-matched wavelengths at the upper end.
  • Battery cells and finished packs. Industry-typical 4–8 weeks, plus documentation time whenever a pack design is new.
  • Custom connectors, pogo pins and charging contacts. Industry-typical 3–8 weeks; the clock is usually tooling, not production.
  • Heating elements and PTC thermistors. Industry-typical 3–6 weeks for standard wattages, with custom dimensions adding tooling time.
  • Silicone and TPU molds. Industry-typical 3–6 weeks for a new tool, then days rather than weeks per production run.

The practical lesson: on a reorder, audit your bill of materials for the three or four parts that are single-sourced and electrically critical. Those are your schedule. Everything else is noise.

The tier you land in is determined by how much of the product you are changing: packaging only, branding plus packaging, or tooling, electronics and enclosure. A reorder of an unchanged SKU sits in the tier it was originally quoted at.

How buyers legitimately move down a tier

  1. Run two or three SKUs on the same platform so the shared components reach a higher combined volume.
  2. Commit to a rolling forecast instead of a single PO, which lets us buy components in larger lots.
  3. Standardize on the standard platform and put your differentiation into packaging and accessories.
  4. Consolidate colors and variants rather than adding a new variant every season.

How long should a reorder take from deposit to FOB Shenzhen?

4–8 weeks for most SKUs at LIGHT BDB, and the low end is only realistic when components are already allocated to your program.

Anyone quoting a flat two weeks on a customized heated massager or red light panel is either holding stock of your exact configuration or being optimistic. The 4–8 week figure is our standard published window, and it assumes a clean reorder with no engineering change.

Where the weeks actually go, industry-typical

  1. Week 0–1. PO confirmation, deposit (T/T 30/70 or L/C at sight), and material release.
  2. Week 1–5. Component procurement — usually the longest single block, and the one that decides whether you land at four weeks or eight.
  3. Week 3–6. SMT, sub-assembly and in-process inspection, which overlap with the tail end of procurement.
  4. Week 5–8. Final assembly, function test, packaging, and FOB handover to your nominated forwarder.

Two levers genuinely shorten this. The first is a blanket order with a scheduled release calendar, which lets us buy long-lead parts before you need finished goods. The second is reusing the exact approved BOM from the previous run instead of re-specifying parts. Samples run 7–14 days and the sample fee is refundable against your PO, which is the cheapest way to lock a BOM before it becomes a production problem.

Do certifications or audits slow down a reorder when a component changes?

Not usually, but a new component supplier or a changed part can trigger a documentation review that adds weeks to the schedule.

LIGHT BDB holds ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL registered factory and Sony Green Partner. Each of those creates an obligation that flows down to the parts we buy, and that obligation does not pause because you are in a hurry.

What each one means for a substitution

  • ISO 9001. A substitute part needs an approved-supplier record and a documented change before it can enter the BOM.
  • ISO 14001. Environmental aspects of a new process or material are reviewed, not assumed.
  • BSCI (amfori). Social compliance expectations extend to the suppliers we onboard.
  • UL registered factory. Changes affecting safety-relevant construction are reviewed against the registered configuration.
  • Sony Green Partner. Controlled chemical substances are screened in the supply chain, so a substitute part must clear the same substance review as the original.

To be straightforward about scope: we are a wellness-device ODM. Those are the certifications we hold and we do not claim anything beyond them. If your sales channel requires a regulated healthcare quality system or a regulatory clearance we do not have, we will tell you that before you place a PO rather than three weeks after.

What are the honest trade-offs between buffering components and ordering just in time?

Buffering components shortens reorders but ties up cash and risks obsolescence; just-in-time protects cash and exposes you to allocation.

There is no version of this where you get both. What you can do is choose deliberately, product by product.

Where the risk actually sits

  • Buffering long-lead ICs. Cuts four to twelve weeks off a reorder, but you are financing inventory and betting the part stays current for two or three seasons.
  • Buffering magnetics. Lower risk for us because we wind them in Panyu, so the "buffer" is really just scheduled production rather than purchased stock.
  • Just-in-time everything. Clean balance sheet, but a single allocation notice from a semiconductor maker can wipe out a launch window.
  • Yangon overflow. Real extra capacity at 5,000+ pcs/day, but it introduces cross-border logistics that a Shenzhen-only run does not have.
  • Higher MOQ tiers. Better unit pricing and earlier component commitments, at the cost of cash and warehouse space.
  • A 500-day limited warranty. One of the longer windows in this category, which means we plan spare-part availability — and that planning feeds back into how we buy components.

The trade-off buyers most often underestimate is engineering change. A buffer of parts you no longer need is worse than no buffer at all, because it converts a schedule problem into a write-off. If your roadmap includes a mid-year refresh, do not let us buy a year of components against the old design.
