What is the difference between FDA registration, 510(k) clearance, and general wellness?

Registration is paperwork about who and where; 510(k) clearance is a review of a specific product; general wellness is a policy line that keeps low-risk products outside that review.

These three terms get used interchangeably in supplier emails, and they answer three completely different questions. Getting them straight before you sign a PO will save you from the most expensive mistake in this category: paying for paperwork that does not mean what you think it means.

  • Establishment registration answers "who makes this, and where?" FDA maintains a public list of facilities that manufacture products it regulates, and each facility lists the product lines it handles. It is renewed on a yearly cycle.
  • 510(k) premarket notification answers "has this specific product, with these specific claims, been reviewed?" The submitter demonstrates substantial equivalence to a legally marketed predicate product, and FDA issues a clearance letter naming that product and that intended use.
  • General wellness answers "does this product need that review at all?" If the labeling stays low-risk and non-disease, the product sits outside the premarket pathway, and nobody files anything.

A way to hold it in your head: registration is about the kitchen, clearance is about the recipe, and general wellness is a judgment that this particular dish does not need a review. Your Shenzhen factory can be fully registered and hold zero product clearances at the same time. Those are not contradictory facts — they are just different columns of the same spreadsheet.

Does FDA establishment registration mean my product can be sold in the US?

No. Registration tells FDA that a facility exists and what it makes; it is not a safety finding, a performance review, or permission to sell.

This is the misunderstanding we correct most often, usually after a buyer has already been shown a PDF with an FDA logo on it. FDA does not issue an "FDA certificate." What most factories forward is a printout of their establishment record or a third-party agent's cover sheet. It is real, it is verifiable, and it says almost nothing about the product you are about to import.

Registration and listing are compliance obligations, not endorsements. A factory registers because it has to, pays the annual fee, lists what it handles, and renews. That is the whole story. Nothing in that process looks at whether your heated massager gets too hot, whether your LED panel's irradiance matches the manual, or whether your box copy makes a claim you cannot support.

How to verify a supplier's FDA paperwork in ten minutes

  1. Find the establishment number. Ask for the facility's registration number in writing, then look it up in FDA's public establishment database. If a supplier cannot give you a number, there is no registration to discuss.
  2. Check that the listed product lines match your SKU. A registration covering unrelated categories is a caution flag, not a green light.
  3. Confirm a US agent is named. Foreign establishments are required to designate one. A missing agent means the registration is incomplete or stale.
  4. Confirm the registration year is current. The update window runs October 1 to December 31. A record from three years ago is a lapsed record.
  5. Ask separately for a clearance number. Clearance numbers follow a "K" plus six digits format and are searchable in FDA's 510(k) database. If nobody can produce a K number, there is no clearance — no matter what the slide deck says.
  6. Ask who owns the clearance. Clearances are held by a specification developer, which is often a brand or a consultant rather than the factory. A factory that "borrows" someone else's clearance for its brochure is telling you something important.

Which wellness products actually need a 510(k) submission?

Only products whose intended use and claims fall into a regulated category need one; most heated massagers, light panels, diffusers, and sleep products do not.

The trigger is labeling, not hardware. The same LED panel, the same vibration motor, the same heating element can sit comfortably inside general wellness or require a premarket submission — the difference is what the box, manual, app screens, and product page say the thing is for. This is why two factories can build near-identical products and only one of them needs a regulatory consultant.

Usually inside general wellness

  • Massage and vibration products positioned around comfort, relaxation, and muscle soothing after exercise.
  • Heated wraps, heated eye massagers, and warming cushions positioned around comfort and winding down.
  • Light and mood products — sunrise lamps, ambient panels, low-level red and near-infrared light for home relaxation routines.
  • Aroma diffusers, white-noise devices, weighted blankets, and sleep accessories without condition-specific claims.

Usually outside general wellness

  • Anything claiming to diagnose, screen for, prevent, or address a named condition or disease.
  • Electrical stimulation products built around pain-relief claims — these sit in a regulated category with an established predicate path.
  • Products with sensor-driven outputs the labeling presents as a health measurement rather than a lifestyle signal.

If your brand plan depends on a claim you cannot drop, the honest answer is that you need a partner with regulated-device capability, and LIGHT BDB is not that partner. We would rather say so in the first call than discover it during a retail compliance review six months later.

What claims keep a heated massager or red light panel inside general wellness?

Claims about relaxation, comfort, muscle soothing after exercise, and the appearance of skin stay inside; claims about named conditions or disease do not.

Reviewers look at the whole package, not one sentence. That means the retail box, the insert, the manual, the listing title and bullets, the app onboarding screens, the warranty page, and the influencer brief you send to creators. A single line in a TikTok script can undo a well-built compliance file.

Language that stays inside the boundary

  • "Helps you unwind after a long day."
  • "Soothes tired muscles after exercise."
  • "Warms the skin and promotes a comfortable, relaxed feeling."
  • "Supports a consistent evening wind-down routine."
  • "Designed for the appearance of skin texture and glow."
  • "A softer, warmer light for reading and resting."

Language that pulls you out of it

  • Naming a condition — arthritis, neuropathy, insomnia, depression, anxiety disorders, fibromyalgia.
  • Implying relief of a symptom rather than comfort — "reduces inflammation," "stops nerve pain," "lowers blood pressure."
  • Healing or recovery claims — "speeds tissue repair," "accelerates wound healing."
  • Measurement claims — "boosts melatonin," "increases circulation by 30%."
  • Comparison to a regulated product — "same technology as the device clinics use for [condition]."

The practical discipline is a claims file: one document that lists every approved sentence, who approved it, and the date, plus a rule that no one ships copy outside it. That file is also what you hand a marketplace compliance team when they ask how you substantiate what the listing says. Brands that maintain one move through retail onboarding noticeably faster in our experience.

Who registers the facility — me or my manufacturer?

Both appear: the foreign factory registers its establishment and lists products, while the US importer or brand owner registers its own establishment and owns the claims.

The factory cannot own your intended use. It can register its building, list the product categories it handles, and name a US agent — but the decision about what the product is "for" belongs to the brand whose name is on the box. That is why the same production line can serve a general-wellness brand and a regulated-claim brand without either of them sharing a compliance posture.

What to put in the supply agreement

  • Which party files and pays for establishment registration, and which party pays the annual fee.
  • Who holds the product listing, and what happens to it if you switch suppliers.
  • A renewal clause: registration lapses quietly, and a lapsed registration is a shipment sitting at port.
  • A labeling control clause — who approves final copy, and how changes get versioned.
  • A document handover list: test reports, safety marks, bills of materials, and the claims file.

For EU and Japanese sales the framework shifts again. The EU approach is stricter in several wellness categories and asks for technical documentation and a risk assessment that the US general-wellness route does not. Japan has its own labeling conventions and product-safety requirements. Budget for a local regulatory reviewer in each market you enter; it is a small line item compared with a relabeling run.

What are the honest risks of the general wellness route?

Claim drift is the biggest one: a single marketing line that names a condition pulls the product out of general wellness and into a review pathway you did not plan for.

Claim drift rarely happens in the manual. It happens in a paid ad written by an agency, a marketplace bullet edited by an intern, or a creator script that "sounds more compelling." The product did not change; the labeling did. Build a review gate for every piece of copy that ships, including the ones you do not think of as labeling.

  • You get no formal document. General wellness gives you a defensible position, not a certificate to hand a retailer. Some retail compliance teams will ask for documentation anyway — the claims file and test reports are what you send.
  • Marketplaces are stricter than the law. Several platforms restrict or reject regulatory status language in listings. Treat "FDA registered" as a phrase to keep out of titles and bullets entirely.
  • Europe and Japan are not the US. A product that is comfortably general wellness in America may need technical documentation and a risk assessment in the EU. Plan per market.
  • Registration lapses silently. It renews annually. If your supplier changes hands or deprioritizes the filing, you find out at customs.
  • Third-party "FDA certificate" sellers exist. There is no such certificate. Anyone selling you one is selling you a printout with an authoritative font.
  • General wellness is not a moat. It is a low barrier, which means your product will be copied. Your defense is the claims file, the safety testing, the 500-day warranty, and the speed of your next iteration — not the regulatory position itself.

None of this argues against the general wellness route. It is the correct route for the overwhelming majority of private-label massagers, light panels, and mood products, and it is why a first-time importer can launch without a regulatory consultant on retainer. It just is not a shortcut past documentation. The brands that scale in this category treat claims discipline as a product feature, not as paperwork — and they tend to be the ones still on the shelf three seasons later.
