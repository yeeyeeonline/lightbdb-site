Battery Products: What Changes in Air Versus Sea Shipping

Quick answer

Air moves lithium battery products as IATA Class 9 dangerous goods, with a 30% state-of-charge cap and cargo-aircraft-only limits for standalone packs. Sea moves the same products under the IMDG Code, with no default SoC cap, lower freight cost and 18–35 extra transit days. LIGHT BDB ships FOB Shenzhen on 4–8 week production lead times.

What actually changes about a battery product when it moves by air instead of by sea?

Four things change: the rulebook, the state of charge, the paperwork and the number of carriers willing to accept the booking.

The product on the pallet is identical. What changes is how the shipment is classified, which instruction set applies, and how much evidence you must produce before anyone loads it. Air is governed by the IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations, which mirror the ICAO Technical Instructions. Sea is governed by the IMDG Code. The two rulebooks agree on the underlying UN 38.3 testing requirement and disagree on almost everything downstream of it.

The classification shifts with the packaging, not the product

This is the detail that catches first-time importers. The same heated massager can travel under three different classifications depending on what else is in the carton:

  • Battery installed in the device — UN3481, "lithium ion batteries contained in equipment." The lightest treatment, and often eligible for Section II provisions with a lithium battery mark rather than a full Class 9 label.
  • Device plus a spare battery in the same box — UN3481, "packed with equipment." Stricter. Section II caps how many spare cells or packs can sit in one package, so a retail bundle with a spare pack can push the whole shipment into fully regulated territory.
  • Loose batteries or power banks — UN3480, "lithium ion batteries in compliance with..." i.e. standalone. By air this is cargo-aircraft-only, with a 30% state-of-charge cap and no Section II exception for lithium-ion. By sea it is routine Class 9 cargo.

The altitude argument behind the air rules

Air freight exposes cells to lower ambient pressure and wider temperature swings than a container does, and a thermal event in a cargo hold is far harder to contain than one on a deck. That is why aviation regulators added the state-of-charge cap and removed the light-touch Section II route for standalone lithium-ion packs. The sea rules lean on packaging strength, segregation and stowage instead.

The booking pool shrinks by air

Not every airline, and not every freight forwarder, accepts Class 9. Fewer still accept UN3480 standalone packs, and some refuse them outright on passenger-connected routings. At sea, most container lines accept Class 9 with correct documentation, but LCL consolidators frequently refuse battery cargo because they cannot segregate it from general freight — so full-container-load, or a specialist LCL consolidator, is often the practical answer.

Which shipping documents change between air and sea for lithium battery cargo?

The core battery documents are the same; the transport document, the hazard labelling and the carrier acceptance check are what change.

Documents both modes require

  • UN 38.3 test summary — the eight-test transport safety report for the cell and pack design, made available to the carrier on request.
  • Safety data sheet (SDS/MSDS) — in the current multi-section format, listing the cell chemistry and Wh rating.
  • Lithium battery mark or Class 9 hazard label — which one depends on whether the shipment qualifies for a small-battery exception or is fully regulated.
  • Commercial invoice and packing list — with the UN number, battery type, Wh or lithium content, and quantity stated explicitly.
  • Wh rating statement — cells at or below 20 Wh and packs at or below 100 Wh generally fall into the small-battery provisions; above those thresholds the shipment is fully regulated.

Air-only additions

  • Shipper's Declaration for Dangerous Goods (DGD) — a signed, edition-specific declaration naming the IATA DGR edition used.
  • Cargo Aircraft Only (CAO) handling — mandatory for UN3480 standalone lithium-ion packs.
  • Function-specific shipper training — the person signing the DGD must be trained to the current IATA DGR, with recurrent training typically every 24 months.
  • Air waybill with the dangerous goods statement — the AWB must carry the "Dangerous Goods as per attached Shipper's Declaration" wording.

Sea-only additions

  • Dangerous goods transport document per the IMDG Code — the multimodal dangerous goods form is normally accepted.
  • Container packing certificate — signed confirmation that the container was packed, segregated and secured to the IMDG Code.
  • Carrier DG approval and stowage category — booked before the container reaches the terminal, not at the gate.
  • Port and vessel DG manifest entries — handled by your forwarder, but the accuracy of the UN number and quantity is your responsibility.

What state of charge should batteries be at for air versus sea?

Air requires 30% state of charge or below for standalone lithium-ion packs; at sea the IMDG Code sets no default cap, though individual carriers may.

The 30% rule targets UN3480 — bare cells and packs travelling on their own. Batteries installed in a finished device (UN3481 contained in equipment) are not covered by that cap in the IATA text, although plenty of forwarders apply a low-SoC policy across the board because it simplifies their screening. If your forwarder asks for 30% on everything, that is a commercial policy, not a regulation, and it is worth knowing the difference when you push back on a booking.

Why the cap exists, and what it costs you

A cell at 30% charge holds less stored energy, so a failure event releases less heat. The operational cost is real: a product shipped by air arrives partly discharged, so retail packaging needs an honest "charge before first use" line, and your QC step has to verify the charge state before the carton is sealed rather than after. Budget for a charge-state check and a discharge step in the packing line, not just a label change.

What sea transit means for cell health

A container crossing to Rotterdam spends four to five weeks in a steel box that can swing from near-freezing nights to 50 °C-plus daytime interior temperatures in summer. Storing cells at a moderate state of charge in a hot container is kinder to cycle life than storing them full, so many battery-savvy brands deliberately ship at 40–60% SoC by sea and add desiccant and thermal buffering. That is an engineering decision, not a compliance one — but it directly affects your field failure rate eighteen months later.

How do air and sea compare on cost for battery-powered products?

Sea is materially cheaper per kilogram; air wins on total cost when the shipment is small and the sales window is short.

Four cost lines move when batteries enter the shipment: the base freight rate, the dangerous goods surcharge, the documentation and training overhead, and the working capital tied up in longer transit. Industry-typical air rates run several times the sea rate per kilogram before the DG surcharge is added, and air forwarders often charge a per-shipment DG handling fee on top. Sea DG surcharges are usually quoted per container rather than per kilogram, which is why sea gets proportionally cheaper as volume rises. Which tests and certificates must exist before either mode is bookable?

UN 38.3 is the one document an airline and a shipping line will both refuse you without.

The UN 38.3 series covers eight abuse conditions — altitude simulation, thermal cycling, vibration, shock, external short circuit, impact or crush, overcharge and forced discharge — and the resulting test summary is the carrier's evidence that the design is transport-safe. It is a design-level document, not a production certificate: change the cell, the protection board or the enclosure and the summary may no longer describe what you are shipping.

Market-access certificates that sit alongside the transport documents

  • IEC 62133-2 — the cell and pack safety standard most buyers and retailers ask for first.
  • UL 2054 / UL 1642 — the pack and cell safety standards commonly required for the US market, granted per design.
  • UL 62368-1 or the applicable end-product standard — for the finished appliance rather than the cell.
  • CE marking — EMC and, where applicable, the Radio Equipment Directive for wireless charging or Bluetooth; LVD where mains adaptors are in the box.
  • UKCA — the parallel UK route for Great Britain.
  • PSE round mark — Japan's mandatory mark for lithium-ion batteries, plus the applicable DENAN requirement for the charger.
  • KC mark — Korea's equivalent for battery products.

Factory credentials versus product approvals

LIGHT BDB holds ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL registered factory status and Sony Green Partner. Those describe how the plant is run and how it is audited — they are not product approvals and they do not authorise transport. What are the honest trade-offs and risks of choosing air or sea?

Air buys speed and costs money, booking friction and a tighter packaging spec; sea buys price and costs time, working capital and carrier flexibility.

Neither mode is a free lunch, and the brands that ship batteries well tend to run both rather than pick a side.

Where air hurts

  • Acceptance risk. A DGD that miscounts Wh ratings or omits the UN number gets rejected at the airline's acceptance desk, and the cargo misses its flight.
  • Capacity risk. Standalone packs need cargo aircraft, and freighter space is finite in peak season.
  • Physics. A heated massager is bulky relative to its value, so you pay to fly air and packaging. Air economics improve as unit value rises and worsen as weight rises.
  • Compliance cost. DGD preparation, trained signatories and per-shipment DG fees are fixed overheads that punish small, frequent shipments.

Where sea hurts

  • Working capital. Four to five extra weeks of transit is four to five extra weeks of inventory you have paid for and cannot sell.
  • Schedule volatility. Blank sailings, port congestion and rerouting are normal, and a DG booking is harder to roll onto the next vessel than a dry container.
  • Consolidation refusal. LCL consolidators often decline Class 9, so small brands can find themselves paying for a full container they did not need.
  • Container heat. Summer interior temperatures age cells. Packaging, desiccant and state of charge at loading all matter.

What we would actually recommend

Ship the launch batch and all samples by air so you hit the retail window with a small, fast, well-documented consignment. Move the replenishment volume by sea on a schedule that assumes a fortnight of slippage. Keep one UN 38.3 summary and one SDS per pack design under version control, and re-check them whenever a cell, protection board or enclosure changes. That single discipline prevents more delayed shipments than any freight negotiation will.

What else do buyers ask about battery shipping by air and sea?

Can battery products be shipped by air at all?

Yes, as Class 9 dangerous goods under the IATA DGR, with a UN 38.3 test summary and a shipper's declaration. Standalone lithium-ion packs are restricted to cargo aircraft and must be at 30% state of charge or below.

Is sea freight cheaper than air for battery products?

Usually by a wide margin — industry-typical air rates run several times the sea rate per kilogram before DG surcharges. The trade-off is 18–35 extra transit days and more capital tied up in stock.

Do I need dangerous goods paperwork for a single sample?

Almost always. A courier parcel containing a lithium-ion battery still needs a UN 38.3 summary and the lithium battery mark, and express carriers screen battery shipments closely. LIGHT BDB's sample turnaround is 7–14 days and the fee is refundable against PO.

What MOQ applies to battery-powered wellness products from LIGHT BDB?

Does a UL registered factory mean my product is UL certified?

No. UL registered factory status is an audit credential for the plant. Product-level marks such as UL 2054 are granted per design and must be tested on the specific pack you ship.
