Which heated products in the range actually fit ski and outdoor retail?

Rechargeable hand warmers, electric heated scarves, heated knee wraps and waist-and-back heating belts fit ski and outdoor retail best; heated eye massagers and abdominal heating belts sell better in lodge and après-ski retail.

The six body-care categories in the LIGHT BDB range split into three natural placements in an outdoor assortment. Think of them as pocket, worn, and lodge. Getting that mapping right before you brief a factory is the difference between a clean first season and a warehouse full of the wrong SKU.

Pocket and glovebox: rechargeable hand warmers

Hand warmers are the impulse item of the category. They sit by the till in resort shops, they go into rental-desk bundles, they get bought by lift operators, ski patrol, snowshoe guides and anyone standing still in a queue at minus ten. Pack size is small, freight cube is low, and the price point is gift-friendly, which is why most outdoor buyers test the category here first.

Many hand warmer designs in this class are built as dual-purpose devices with a USB output so they can top up a phone on the mountain. If you want that feature, spec it at sampling, because it changes the cell, the port layout and the compliance file. Keep the retail copy honest: it is a warmer that can also charge, not a power bank that happens to heat.

Worn warmth: scarves, knee wraps, waist-and-back belts

Electric heated scarves are the easiest apparel-adjacent sell in the range because they are effectively size-free. One SKU covers most of your adult customers, which means a 500 pcs first order can sit across two or three colorways without a sizing matrix.

Heated knee wraps perform well with instructors, patrol staff, snowshoe and fat-bike riders, and the growing crowd who sit outside at cafés in winter. They are also a strong add-on sale next to a boot or binding purchase, because the customer is already thinking about their legs.

Waist-and-back heating belts are the stadium and outdoor-dining item. Lift queues, terrace lunches, cold-weather spectator sports, winter markets. Unlike scarves, belts need a size run, so plan two to four sizes as a design decision at sampling rather than something you bolt on later.

Lodge and recovery: eye massagers and abdominal belts

Heated eye massagers and abdominal heating belts do not go up the mountain. They go in the room. That means lodge retail, hotel gift shops, chalet welcome amenities, spa retail and the travel-retail shelf at the airport on the way home. Position them for wind-down and travel comfort, not for the slope, and they make sense in the same door as the scarves without cannibalising them.

What MOQ should a first-time outdoor brand expect for heated wearables?

Body-care MOQ starts at 500 pcs for standard builds, 1,000 pcs for white-label and 2,000–3,000 pcs for full ODM custom work.

Those three tiers exist for a practical reason: they map to how much tooling, packaging and line-change time your order consumes. A standard build in our existing shell and colorway can run at 500 pcs. Put your logo on the product and a retail-ready box around it and you are in white-label territory at 1,000 pcs. Change the shell, the heating zone layout or the control interface and you are in ODM custom work at 2,000–3,000 pcs, because the factory now has to build and validate something that did not exist before.

For a first season, the smartest structure is usually one hero SKU at 500 pcs in a standard build, with your brand on the packaging rather than moulded into the product. Prove the sell-through, then move to white-label and a wider colorway range in year two. Two mediocre SKUs at 500 pcs each is a much harder first order to recover from than one that works.

  • Standard, 500 pcs: existing shell, existing colorways, our packaging or a simple branded sleeve.
  • White-label, 1,000 pcs: your logo on the product, your retail box, your manual and warning copy.
  • ODM custom, 2,000–3,000 pcs: new shell, new heating zone layout, new control scheme, custom accessories.

How do heated scarves and hand warmers hold up in real sub-zero conditions?

They hold up best when the battery is sized for cold, not for a showroom: industry-typical heated wearables lose roughly 30–50% of their runtime below freezing compared with a 20°C bench test.

That single number causes more returns in this category than any other factor. A scarf that runs six hours on a lab bench may run three to four hours on a chairlift at minus eight, because lithium cells derate in the cold and the heater is working against a much larger temperature delta. Your spec sheet should always state runtime at a cold ambient temperature, not just at room temperature, and your packaging copy should match.

What to specify at sampling

  • Cell capacity and chemistry — the runtime you promise is set here, not in the firmware.
  • Low-temperature behaviour — how the controller behaves when the pack is cold, and whether output tapers or cuts.
  • Heat zone layout — a small zone that gets hot reads better in a shop than a large zone that gets warm, but the large zone gets fewer complaints.
  • Charge time and port type — USB-C is now the default expectation in outdoor retail; micro-USB reads as old stock.
  • Removability — if you want a scarf that can be laundered, the electronics and pack must come out. That is a design decision you make before tooling, not after.
  • Ingress protection — decide your IP target early. Most heated wearables in this class are built splash-resistant rather than submersible, and snow exposure is not the same as a river crossing.

Ask for a repeatable test protocol with the sample: same ambient temperature, same starting charge, same heat setting, logged runtime. If a supplier cannot give you that, you cannot write an honest spec sheet.

Which certifications and compliance documents does the outdoor channel ask for?

On the factory side, LIGHT BDB holds ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL registered factory status and Sony Green Partner; product compliance is separate and is built per SKU.

Buyers new to importing often conflate the two. Factory-management certifications tell your QA team that the plant runs documented processes, environmental controls and audited labour conditions. They do not certify that a specific scarf is legal to sell in your market. That comes from testing your SKU against the rules of the destination country, and it is your file to own, usually through your own test house.

What each market typically asks for

  • EU: CE marking supported by the relevant directives, RoHS, REACH, plus battery and packaging obligations. WEEE registration sits with you as the importer of record.
  • UK: UKCA in place of CE, with the same underlying test evidence in most cases.
  • US: FCC rules for anything with a digital circuit or radio, plus state-level battery and e-waste rules that vary.
  • Japan: PSE for the charger side, plus local labelling and importer details.
  • Air freight: UN38.3 test documentation for the lithium cells, and a forwarder who will accept the pack.

One thing worth saying plainly: these are comfort and warmth products. They are sold on how they feel in cold weather, not on health outcomes. Keep your packaging copy in that lane and your compliance life gets much simpler.

How should a buyer plan sampling and production around a ski season?

Work backwards from your shelf date: samples take 7–14 days, then production runs 4–8 weeks from deposit to FOB Shenzhen for most SKUs.

Add your own buffers on both ends. Inspection, consolidation, sea freight to the US or EU, and customs clearance can easily consume four to six weeks more, which is why most outdoor buyers in this category place winter POs in late spring rather than late summer. If your shelf date is October and you want stock in the DC by September, a May deposit is comfortable and a July deposit is tight.

A workable calendar

  1. January–March: shortlist categories, request samples, run your own cold-weather runtime test.
  2. March–April: lock the spec, freeze packaging artwork, confirm the compliance test plan with your lab.
  3. May–June: place the PO, pay the 30% deposit, book the forwarder.
  4. June–August: production and pre-shipment inspection; 4–8 weeks from deposit.
  5. August–September: freight, customs, DC receipt.
  6. October: on shelf ahead of the first snow.

Payment terms support that rhythm: T/T 30/70 or L/C at sight, FOB Shenzhen, with your nominated forwarder. If you are running an L/C, start the paperwork earlier than you think you need to, because bank discrepancies are the most common cause of a slipped sailing.

What are the honest trade-offs of taking heated wearables into the ski channel?

The biggest one is coverage: LIGHT BDB does not make heated gloves, socks, insoles, mittens or vest liners, so a full "heated apparel wall" needs a second supplier.

That is a real limitation, not a marketing gap. Those items are industry-typical channel stock made by other factories, and if you want them you will be managing two compliance files, two MOQ commitments and two production calendars. Some buyers deliberately avoid that and build their assortment around the six categories we do make; others accept the complexity because the wall looks better full. Both are defensible, and you should decide before you start briefing.

The other trade-offs worth naming

  • Battery derating is unforgiving. Promise a cold-weather runtime you have actually measured, or plan for returns and chargebacks.
  • Hand warmers are commoditised. Price shopping is fierce and differentiation is hard. They win the first order and the scarf or belt wins the margin.
  • 500 pcs is real money. Body-care MOQ is higher than the Light & Mood platforms for good reason, but a first-time importer should not spread it across four SKUs.
  • One winter window. Miss October and the stock ages a full year. Seasonality risk sits with you, not the factory.
  • Product compliance is yours. Factory certifications are not a substitute for testing your SKU in your market.
  • A 500-day warranty needs planning. It is one of the longest in the category and a genuine selling point, but budget for spare packs and a returns process to match it.
  • Sizing is a decision, not a detail. Belts need a size run; scarves and hand warmers do not. That changes your MOQ maths per SKU.

None of this argues against the category. It argues for choosing two SKUs, testing them properly, and building the wall in year two once you know which one your customer actually reaches for.
