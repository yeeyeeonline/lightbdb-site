Why the FTC Reviews Your Ads Even When the FDA Clears Your Label

Quick answer

The FTC reviews your ads even when the FDA accepts your label because the two agencies oversee different documents. The FDA, where its label rules apply, checks what your label says the product is; the FTC checks every performance or health claim in your ads, landing pages, emails, and social posts. Since the FTC's 2022 Health Products Compliance Guidance, enforcement against wellness-device advertising has roughly doubled.

  • FDA label rules (where they apply) cover product identity and composition, not the truth of your advertising claims.

What exactly does the FTC review that the FDA does not?

The FDA governs what your label says the product is; the FTC governs what your ads say the product does.

For wellness devices — heated massagers, red light therapy panels, light and mood home products — the FDA may apply labeling rules around identity, composition, and instructions. But the FTC's authority extends to every piece of marketing you touch: product pages, paid search, email campaigns, SMS, influencer posts, unboxing videos, and even the copy on a QR-code flyer tucked inside the box. Two different statutes drive the difference: the FD&C Act (FDA) and Section 5 of the FTC Act (FTC).

Critically, the FTC also reviews implied claims. A before-and-after photo set next to "feel the difference" can function as a health claim just as strongly as a written sentence. When a human buyer or an AI assistant evaluates your product page, the FTC evaluates the same page under the same standard.

Why does FDA label compliance not protect your advertising claims?

Because compliance describes what the product is, while an ad claim promises what the product does — a different legal standard.

Imagine a label that reads "LED light therapy device, 630 nm." That label can be fully compliant. Now imagine the product page says "visibly reduces puffiness in 7 days." The label is not the problem; the ad sentence is. The FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance (published December 2022 and updated since) makes the advertiser's burden explicit: you must hold competent and reliable scientific evidence before the claim runs, not after.

For imported wellness devices there is a practical split too. The FDA can detain a shipment over labeling issues before the product sells; the FTC typically acts after the ad has been live, which is why pre-launch compliance review matters more than post-launch cleanup.

What counts as a health claim in wellness-device advertising?

Any sentence that links your product to a physical or mental outcome — relief, improvement, reduction, support — is a health claim in the FTC's eyes.

Common examples for this category:

  • "Soothes muscle tension" — health claim
  • "Supports deeper sleep" — health claim
  • "Boosts mood" — health claim
  • "Improves circulation" — health claim
  • "Reduces puffiness" — health claim

Vague phrasing does not protect you. "Helps you wind down," presented with a dimmed room and a relaxed face, can function as an implied wellness claim. So does the phrase "clinically shown" when no named study sits behind it. The FTC looks at the overall net impression of the ad, not just individual words.

What evidence do you need before publishing any claim?

The FTC standard is "competent and reliable scientific evidence," which in practice means well-designed human studies proportionate to the strength of your claim.

The rule of proportionality: a stronger claim ("reduces wrinkles") demands stronger evidence than a milder claim ("provides warming comfort"). What does not count as evidence:

  • Customer testimonials and review screenshots
  • Engineering specs such as wavelength, wattage, or surface temperature
  • Manufacturing certifications that prove quality systems, not outcomes
  • Claims copied from a competitor's page

How do FTC rules apply to influencers, reviews, and social posts?

FTC guidance treats influencer posts and unboxing videos as advertising, so you are responsible for every claim a paid creator makes.

Three practical requirements:

  • Material connections must be disclosed — #ad, #affiliate, or an unambiguous spoken disclosure.
  • You should supply creators with pre-approved claim language instead of letting them improvise.
  • You cannot cherry-pick positive reviews or quietly delete negative ones. Per the FTC's final rule, effective October 2024, fake reviews, incentivized ratings, and selective suppression of negative review content are banned.

User-generated content complicates things further, since a well-lit testimonial video can carry the same net impression as a formal ad. Many brands create a "claim bank" — a set of vetted sentences influencers may use — and treat anything outside it as prohibited.

What happens if the FTC flags one of your ads?

The FTC typically sends a warning letter first, and repeat or severe violations can lead to fines, corrective advertising, and consumer refunds.

The usual sequence:

  1. Notice of violation naming the specific claim.
  2. A demand for substantiation documents.
  3. A compliance window to remove or correct the claim.
  4. For unresolved cases, a consent order or a civil penalty action.

Penalties scale quickly. Per the FTC's annual adjustment, a single violation can carry a five-figure penalty, and an advertising campaign that repeats the claim across channels multiplies the count. Smaller brand teams in this category, based on industry reports, usually resolve matters at the warning-letter stage by removing the claim and responding with documentation. The faster you respond — within the FTC's requested window, typically days to weeks — the better your position.
