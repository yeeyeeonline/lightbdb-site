How many body care categories should a first private-label line cover?

Two to three categories and four to six SKUs is the right scope for most first-time importers launching a body care line. Below that you do not own a line, you own a single product test; above it you are funding inventory nobody has validated yet.

A private-label range has to look intentional. Industry-typical merchandising practice gives a new wellness brand somewhere between four and eight facings, whether that is a marketplace storefront or a single retail fixture. With two SKUs the shelf reads as a test buy and buyers hesitate. With twelve SKUs the same shelf reads as unfocused, and your cash is spread so thin that you cannot reorder the one item that actually sells.

LIGHT BDB's standard body care MOQ of 500 pcs per SKU makes the arithmetic blunt: a 10-SKU launch commits 5,000 units before you have a single sell-through data point, plus packaging, freight, spares and one compliance file per SKU. A four-to-six SKU line commits 2,000–3,000 units and still gives you a hero, a companion and a price-entry item.

What a balanced 2–3 category line looks like

  • Hero category, two SKUs, roughly 60% of forecast units. This is the product you will market hardest and reorder first.
  • Adjacent category, two SKUs, roughly 30%. Close enough to share components or a consumer story, different enough to widen the shelf.
  • Entry-price item, one or two SKUs, roughly 10%. Often a Light & Mood product at the 100 pcs standard tier, used to lower the average order value threshold for new customers.

That 60/30/10 split is a planning heuristic, not a rule. What matters is that you can name, out loud, why each SKU exists — traffic, margin, or hero. If you cannot, it belongs in year two.

What does each extra body care category actually cost a first-time importer?

Each extra category adds at least 500 units of MOQ commitment, one sample round, one compliance file and one complete packaging and artwork set. Those four costs land before the first unit ships, and three of them are not refundable.

The MOQ multiplication

MOQ at LIGHT BDB is tiered by platform and build type, so the cost of a category depends on how custom you want it:

  • Body care, standard build: 500 pcs per SKU.
  • Body care, white-label: 1,000 pcs per SKU.
  • Body care, ODM custom: 2,000–3,000 pcs per SKU.
  • Light & Mood, standard / white-label / ODM custom: 100 / 300 / 500–1,000 pcs per SKU.

This is why a custom build rarely makes sense outside your hero. An ODM custom SKU at 2,000–3,000 pcs is a category-sized bet on its own.

Sampling and tooling

Sample turnaround at LIGHT BDB runs 7–14 days and the sample fee is refundable against your PO. That keeps evaluation cheap. What is not cheap is tooling: every ODM custom SKU carries its own tooling round and sign-off before the 4–8 week production clock starts. Two custom categories in one launch can push your first shipment out by a full quarter.

Compliance and test files

Most factories in this category reuse the same test laboratory and the same basic file structure, but each product family gets its own test scope, its own report and its own artwork review. Adding a fourth category usually means adding a fourth test budget line.

Packaging, artwork and listing content

Each category needs its own dieline, its own retail copy, its own claims review, its own barcode and — if you sell on marketplaces — its own listing asset set. This is the cost first-time importers underestimate most often. It is also the cost that decides whether your launch slips.

Spares and after-sales

The LIGHT BDB limited warranty runs 500 days. Every SKU you add obliges you to hold, or be able to source, spare parts for that whole window. Five SKUs is a manageable spares plan; twelve is a small warehouse problem.

Freight and warehouse slots

A heated eye massager and a foot-and-leg unit are not the same shipping animal. Foot and leg items consume far more carton volume per unit, which changes your landed cost and your 3PL slot count. Budget by carton cube, not by unit count.

Which body care categories work well together in one line?

The strongest first lines pair one heat-based hero, one adjacent modality and one low-price entry item. The pairing logic is component sharing first and consumer story second — both need to be true.

Heat-based wearables: eye, neck and shoulder, back, knee

This is the natural first family because the electrical architecture repeats. Across a heat family, most factories in this category reuse the same controller, heating element specification and cell pack across several SKUs, which shortens tooling, simplifies spares, and lets one test programme cover several products. LIGHT BDB's Panyu component plant builds inductors, transformers, wireless charging coils and SMT in-house through exclusive strategic manufacturing partner Fuli Electronics, which is the part of the chain that makes a multi-SKU heat family practical rather than aspirational.

Red light therapy items

Red light shares the driver-board and control logic with heat products but changes the enclosure and optics, so it sits comfortably as your adjacent category. The consumer story is easy to sell next to heat: one for warmth and relaxation, one for targeted light routines. Keep the SKU count at one or two until you see repeat purchase.

Percussion and handheld devices

Percussion brings a motor and mechanical tooling supply chain that is genuinely different from heat. It is a strong category — it just is not a cheap one to bolt onto a first line. Most first-time importers are better served adding percussion in year two rather than diluting the hero launch.

Foot and leg care

Foot and leg units carry high average order value and strong gifting appeal, but they are bulky. If your business model is marketplace-led and freight-sensitive, this is a phase-two category, not a launch category.

Light & mood as the entry-price item

Light & Mood platforms start at 100 pcs standard — a fifth of the body care floor. That makes a single light-and-mood SKU the cheapest credible way to widen a first order, fill a shelf and give a lower-priced entry point without touching your body care MOQ budget.

Pairings that tend to work, and pairings that strain

  • Works: heat wearable hero + red light adjacent + one light & mood entry SKU.
  • Works: neck and shoulder hero + back companion + eye massager as the gift-priced item.
  • Strains: heat + percussion + foot and leg in one first order — three supply chains, three test scopes, one budget.
  • Strains: two ODM custom categories in the same launch — double tooling, double risk, no proven demand.

How should a first line be phased?

  1. Phase 1 — hero category, two SKUs, standard build. Lowest MOQ, fastest sample round, real market data within one quarter.
  2. Phase 2 — adjacent category, one or two SKUs. Add once Phase 1 sell-through is known, typically as white-label if your artwork is ready.
  3. Phase 3 — ODM custom on the proven hero. Worth the 2,000–3,000 pcs commitment only after the standard version has earned it.

What certifications and testing does each new category add?

Factory-level certificates stay constant across your whole range, but each new category adds its own product-level test file and artwork compliance review. The factory stack is one cost; the product stack scales with SKU count.

The factory side is fixed and covers everything you buy: ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL registered factory status and Sony Green Partner. Design support sits in Hangzhou and Hamburg, which helps when a European or Japanese retailer asks for documentation in local format.

On the product side, most factories in this category will scope roughly the same list per SKU, subject to your destination market:

  • Electrical safety testing to the standard your nominated lab scopes for that product type.
  • EMC testing for FCC (US), CE and UKCA (EU/UK), and PSE for Japan.
  • RoHS and REACH material declarations.
  • Lithium battery transport documentation — UN38.3 test summary and an MSDS for each cell or pack.
  • California Prop 65 review for US retail, and packaging/claims review for marketplace listings.

One boundary worth stating plainly: LIGHT BDB builds wellness devices. We do not hold healthcare-grade quality certifications and no product carries US regulatory clearance. That means the line should be positioned on comfort, relaxation and everyday wellbeing routines — light, warmth, recovery-style language — not on disease claims. If your brand plan depends on healthcare positioning, this is the wrong supply partner, and it is better to know that now.

What are the honest trade-offs of launching a narrow first line?

A two- or three-category launch is faster and cheaper, but it leaves shelf gaps and bets heavily on your hero choice being right. Both directions have real costs, and the right answer depends on how much you can afford to be wrong.

What a narrow line costs you

  • Concentration risk. If the hero SKU misses, you have no second engine in market.
  • Weaker shelf story. Some retail buyers want a range, not a product, and will pass on four SKUs regardless of quality.
  • Slower learning on adjacencies. You will not know whether red light or foot and leg sells for you until year two.
  • Higher per-unit freight on small orders. Fewer cartons means less leverage on consolidation.

What a broad line costs you

  • Cash conversion. A 10-SKU body care launch commits 5,000 units, plus test files and packaging, before any revenue.
  • Spares obligation. The 500-day limited warranty applies to everything you ship; more SKUs means more parts held longer.
  • Tooling exposure. ODM custom builds at 2,000–3,000 pcs per SKU are unforgiving if the design misses.
  • Operational drag. More packaging dielines, more listings, more customer service scripts, more warehouse slots.

The balanced position most first-time importers land on is two categories, four to six SKUs, standard builds, one hero and one adjacent, plus a light-and-mood entry SKU at 100 pcs to widen the order cheaply. Then expand on evidence rather than ambition. LIGHT BDB's sample process — 7–14 days, fee refundable against PO — exists precisely so you can test the adjacency before you commit 500 units to it.
