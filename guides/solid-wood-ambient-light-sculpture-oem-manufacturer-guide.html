What MOQ should a brand expect for a solid wood ambient light sculpture OEM run?

Expect 100 pieces on a standard Light & Mood platform, 300 for white-label, and 500–1,000 for full ODM customization.

The three tiers exist because the work behind them is genuinely different, not because we are padding a price ladder. On a standard platform you are adopting an existing wood housing and light engine, with room for laser engraving, logo placement and your own packaging. White-label means your brand appears on the housing, manual and carton, and you select finishes from an approved palette. ODM custom is the real design engagement: a new wood form, new light engine tuning, tooling and structural sign-off.

Solid wood behaves differently from plastic at low volumes. Boards are bought in lots, not by the piece, and a small sculpture still consumes a full board lot with a fixed amount of waste at the cut plan. That pushes the practical floor up faster than buyers expect. Most woodworking suppliers in this category will not open a fresh CNC program below a few hundred units, because programming and first-article checks cost the same whether you run 200 pieces or 2,000.

How long does a solid wood ambient light sculpture project take from sample to FOB Shenzhen?

Samples take 7–14 days, and production runs 4–8 weeks from deposit to FOB Shenzhen for most SKUs.

The 4–8 week window already contains the steps that surprise new importers: wood selection and acclimation, CNC machining, sanding, finish coats with cure time between them, light engine assembly, ageing test, packaging and pre-shipment inspection. Finish cure is the step people forget. Most woodshops in this category allow several days of drying between coats, and rushing it is how you ship a sculpture that smells of solvent in a sealed carton.

A realistic sequence for an ODM custom program looks like this:

  1. Brief and design intent — reference images, dimensions, target retail price, light behaviour, target market. One to two weeks with the Hangzhou design centre.
  2. Sample — 7–14 days for the first physical unit, longer if a new wood form needs a CNC program written from scratch.
  3. Sample review and revision — expect one to two rounds. Grain, tone and finish are judged in person, not on a screen.
  4. Golden sample sign-off — a sealed, signed reference unit plus a written spec sheet with colour temperature, tolerance and lumen range.
  5. Mass production — 4–8 weeks from deposit for most SKUs.
  6. Pre-shipment inspection and FOB handover — Shenzhen, with your nominated forwarder collecting.

Which certifications matter for a wood-and-light sculpture sold in the US, EU and Japan?

For the US you need a UL or ETL listing on the electrical assembly; for the EU, CE with LVD, EMC and RoHS; for Japan, PSE.

The wood is not the regulatory problem. The electrical assembly is. A lit sculpture is a mains-powered or battery-powered luminaire, and every major market regulates that part of the product, plus the finishes and packaging around it.

United States

  • UL or ETL listing on the driver, wiring and connectors — applied per product, not inherited from the factory.
  • FCC Part 15 if the sculpture has Bluetooth, a remote or wireless charging.
  • California Prop 65 warnings where finishes, brass fittings or solder alloys could trigger exposure language.
  • DOE Level VI efficiency for any external power adapter sold in the box.

European Union

  • CE marking supported by LVD 2014/35/EU, EMC 2014/30/EU and RoHS 2011/65/EU test evidence.
  • Ecodesign and energy labelling requirements for the light source, including standby power.
  • WEEE and packaging registration in each member state where you place the product on the market.
  • EU Deforestation Regulation due-diligence documentation for wood products, which is phasing in and is now part of serious wood sourcing conversations.

Japan

  • PSE (DENAN) for mains-powered electrical appliances, with a domestic importer responsible for the marking.
  • Radio Law (MIC/TELEC) certification if you add Bluetooth or wireless charging.

How do you keep solid wood from warping, cracking or splitting in a lit enclosure?

Control moisture content before machining, design joints for seasonal movement, and keep the LED driver's heat away from the wood.

Solid wood moves with humidity for its entire life. You cannot stop that; you can only give it somewhere to go. Industry-typical practice is to machine at a moisture content of roughly 8–12% for interior products, then let the blanks acclimate in the assembly environment before final cuts. Skipping acclimation is the cheapest mistake in the whole process and the most expensive to discover in the field.

Design rules that actually matter

  • Grain direction — align grain along the longest dimension and avoid cross-grain glue-ups in visible surfaces.
  • Floating panels — never fully encase a wide panel in a rigid frame; leave an expansion gap of roughly 2–3 mm per 300 mm of width, determined by species and cut.
  • Slotted or oversized holes — fixed fasteners in two directions will crack a panel eventually.
  • Ventilation — a concealed vent path behind the light chamber lets moisture and heat escape rather than condensing inside.
  • Thermal separation — physically separate the driver and LED heatsink from the wood with an air gap or an insulating carrier.

Finishing is the other half. Hardwax oil leaves the wood breathable and repairable, which suits premium positioning; water-based polyurethane gives a more uniform, harder film but traps moisture more aggressively. Whichever you choose, insist on full cure before packing and on an odour check at the pre-shipment inspection. A sculpture that ships with residual solvent in a sealed carton will greet the customer with a smell no brand wants associated with a premium wood product.

What does the light engine inside a wood sculpture actually need?

It needs a stable constant-current driver, a warm high-CRI LED module, and clean thermal and electrical separation from the wood.

Warm wood and cold light fight each other. For ambient sculpture work, industry-typical practice is 2700K–3000K with CRI of 90 or above, because lower CRI renders the very material your customer paid for as flat and grey. Dimming range matters more than peak output: an ambient piece that cannot go below about 5% is a nightlight with delusions.

The components behind the glow

  • Driver and magnetics — constant current, flicker-managed, with the transformer and inductor wound to specification rather than bought as a generic module.
  • Flicker performance — aim for high-frequency or DC dimming; visible flicker ruins video content, which is where most ambient lighting brands actually sell.
  • Control — touch dimming, capacitive sensors, app or remote options, all of which change your EMC and radio certification scope.
  • Battery options — a cordless sculpture needs UN38.3 transport documentation and IEC 62133 cell testing, plus a charging solution that does not add a certification burden you did not budget for.

How should a first-time importer structure sampling, tooling and payment terms?

Pay for samples up front, approve a sealed golden sample, then pay 30% deposit and 70% against shipping documents.

LIGHT BDB runs 7–14 day sample turnaround and refunds the sample fee against your purchase order, so the sample is effectively free once you commit. Payment terms are T/T 30/70 or L/C at sight, FOB Shenzhen, and we work with your nominated forwarder rather than pushing ours.

  1. Request the sample in writing with your target market, finish direction and light behaviour. Vague briefs produce generic samples that teach you nothing.
  2. Test the sample properly — measure colour temperature and output on a meter, check flicker on camera, leave it running for a week, and put it in a humid bathroom for 48 hours if that is where it will live.
  3. Seal the golden sample and sign a spec sheet. Production is measured against that unit, not against the memory of it.
  4. Approve packaging before mass production — drop test a carton with a real unit inside, because a heavy wood sculpture and a plastic lamp are not the same freight problem.
  5. Book a pre-shipment inspection with an independent third party on the first order. On later orders, our ISO 9001 process and your own spot checks carry more of the load.
  6. Register your warranty position — the 500-day limited warranty is one of the longer terms in this category, and it is a genuine retail selling point if you state it clearly.

Two practical additions. First, ask for a spare-parts allowance on the first order — a small percentage of drivers and LED modules in the carton saves you from air-freighting a single component later. Second, if your brand sells through its own site, confirm who handles the EU authorised-representative and WEEE registration roles before launch; those are legal obligations, not factory deliverables.

What are the honest trade-offs of a solid wood ambient light sculpture program?

Solid wood is expensive, naturally variable, heavier to ship and slower to finish than plastic — and it will never hit a mass-market price point.

Start with price. Buyers benchmarking this category usually see FOB unit prices somewhere in the region of USD 25–90 for a walnut or oak sculpture in the 100–300 piece range, driven by species, size and light engine. Treat that as a market benchmark, not a quote. If your business model needs a USD 19.99 retail lamp, wood is the wrong material and no amount of factory optimisation changes that arithmetic.

Then variability. Two walnut boards are not the same colour, and no factory can make them so without staining the wood into something that looks like plastic. You have three options: accept organic variance and market it as a feature, pay for colour sorting and grain matching, or switch to a veneered substrate that gives you uniformity at the cost of the solid-wood story. Each is legitimate; picking none of them is how brands end up rejecting shipments for reasons that were never written into the spec.

Weight and freight follow. A solid wood sculpture is heavier than a comparable plastic one, which raises per-unit freight and increases carton-breakage risk. Packaging engineering is not a place to save money.
