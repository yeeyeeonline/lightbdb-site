General Wellness vs Regulated Device: Where the Line Sits for Private-Label Brands

Quick answer

The line sits on intended use, not on hardware. Market a heated massager or red-light panel for comfort, relaxation or appearance and it stays general wellness; claim it addresses a named condition and regulators reclassify it — an industry-typical 6 to 18 months of extra filing work before you can sell.

What actually decides whether a product is general wellness or a regulated device?

Intended use decides it — the claims you publish, the population you name and the outcome you promise, not the components inside.

This is the single most expensive misunderstanding in private-label sourcing. Two brands can build the identical heated massager from the same BOM at the same factory. One sells it as a relaxation product and ships in eight weeks. The other writes "relieves chronic back pain" on the box and discovers it is now in a regulated pathway with testing, documentation and market registration attached.

The three inputs reviewers actually read

  1. The claim list. Packaging copy, listing bullets, the A+ content panel, the instruction manual, the app screens, the influencer brief and the customer-service script. Regulators and plaintiff lawyers read all of it, and a marketplace listing usually gets read first.
  2. The intended-use sentence. The first paragraph of your instructions is effectively a legal document. "For relaxation and comfort" is a different product from "for the management of musculoskeletal pain."
  3. The named audience. "For adults who sit at a desk all day" reads as wellness. "For people with arthritis" names a condition and pulls the product across the line, even if the hardware never changes.

How the three big markets frame it

In the US, the FDA's general wellness guidance is the reference point. It looks for two things: an intended use that relates only to general health or a healthy lifestyle, and low risk to users. Crucially, the guidance expects no reference to specific diseases or conditions. Keep disease out of the copy and a low-power heated massager stays comfortably inside the policy.

In the EU, classification follows the intended purpose the manufacturer states under the MDR framework. Borderline products — light devices, massage devices, cosmetic-adjacent hardware — are decided on the wording, not the wattage. Cosmetic claims stay outside; prevention, monitoring or management of a disease brings the product into scope, with a notified body in the loop for most classes.

In Japan, the PMD Act splits products into general goods, controlled products and specially controlled products. Most wellness hardware enters as general goods, which is a comparatively light pathway — but only for as long as the claims stay generic. The moment the copy promises an effect on a named condition, the product moves up a tier and the paperwork multiplies.

Which claims keep a heated massager in the wellness lane?

Comfort, relaxation, warmth and appearance claims keep a heated massager in the wellness lane; condition-specific claims about pain, disease or diagnosis move it out.

Language that stays in wellness

  • "Warms and relaxes tired muscles after a long day."
  • "Soothing warmth for your evening wind-down routine."
  • "Helps you unwind at your desk."
  • "Promotes the appearance of smoother-looking skin." (cosmetic-adjacent, not condition-specific)
  • "Designed for comfort and relaxation."

Language that pulls you out

  • Any verb that promises to relieve, reduce, improve or manage a named condition.
  • Disease names, including common ones: arthritis, sciatica, insomnia, seasonal affective disorder, neuropathy.
  • Prevention framing — "prevents", "protects against", "reduces your risk of".
  • Diagnostic framing — "detects", "assesses", "screens for".
  • Comparative efficacy against a regulated product or a drug.

The word "therapy" is not automatically a problem

"Light therapy" and "heat therapy" are descriptive category terms that shoppers search for, and many wellness brands keep them in the product name. The risk is not the noun, it is the verb attached to it. "Light therapy device for relaxation" reads very differently from "light therapy that reverses skin damage." If your legal reviewer is cautious, keep the category word and strip every outcome verb.

Where copy hides

Most classification accidents happen after launch, in places the brand forgot: the FAQ tab on the listing, the back of the manual, the warranty card, a support macro, a paid influencer's script, a push notification from the companion app. Treat every one of those surfaces as regulated copy and route it through the same review as the packaging.

What changes operationally once a product crosses into a regulated pathway?

Everything gets heavier: an industry-typical regulated launch adds 6 to 18 months and five to low-six figures before the first sale.

The production itself barely changes. What changes is the paper around it — a quality system built to the medical standard, technical documentation, human-use evidence, labelling with the right symbols, a local authorised representative, market registration and post-market surveillance obligations that continue for the life of the product. Those costs are mostly fixed, which is why regulated products need far higher volumes to amortise them than a 500-piece wellness run.

What the table means for a first order

The practical consequence is volume math. A regulated pathway with fixed documentation costs only makes sense at volumes well above what a first-time importer typically orders. If your business plan starts at 300 to 1,000 pieces, the wellness lane is not a compromise — it is the only commercially coherent entry point.

Where do red light panels and light-and-mood products usually land?

Most red light panels and mood lamps sit in the wellness lane when marketed for appearance, relaxation and ambience, and leave it the moment copy names a condition.

Appearance claims

"Promotes the appearance of firmer-looking skin" is cosmetic-adjacent language and stays in wellness. "Reduces wrinkles", "reverses photoaging" or "stimulates collagen production" starts making physiological promises that regulators read as therapeutic. The gap between those two sentences is where most red-light brands get into trouble.

Mood and light claims

"Warm ambient light for your evening routine" is safe. Naming seasonal affective disorder, depression or anxiety moves the product out of the wellness lane in every major market. "Supports a calm bedtime routine" is fine; "helps you sleep through the night" edges toward a physiological claim and deserves a second look from your reviewer.

Technical claims are not a safe harbour

Quoting wavelength, irradiance or joules per centimetre does not make a product regulated on its own — but a technical spec sheet that implies a therapeutic outcome does. Publish performance data as specification, not as promised results.

What should a first-time importer put in writing before tooling starts?

Put five things in writing before tooling: target markets, the approved claim list, label artwork, legal-manufacturer and importer roles, and who owns the technical file.

  1. Target markets, in priority order. US, EU and Japan diverge enough on claims that the same copy deck will not clear all three. Decide which market ships first and build the copy around it.
  2. An approved claim list, signed off internally. One page, one owner. Everything published — packaging, listing, manual, app, support scripts — draws from that page and nothing else.
  3. Label artwork with the correct local requirements. EU labels need an importer name and address; US labels need the right regulatory disclaimers for your claim set. These are cheap to fix before plates are cut and expensive after.
  4. Roles: who is the legal manufacturer, who is the importer of record. In most private-label arrangements the brand is the specification developer even though the factory builds the product. Write that down explicitly.
  5. Ownership of the technical file. Confirm that build documentation, test reports and component declarations are handed to you and stay yours if you switch suppliers.

What are the honest trade-offs of staying in the wellness lane?

Staying in wellness buys speed and lower cost, but you give up strong efficacy language, some retail shelf space, and any defensible performance story.

  • Competitors will out-claim you. Listings promising dramatic outcomes will sometimes rank better. They also carry removal and litigation risk that you are choosing not to take.
  • Some buyers want substantiation. Larger retailers and pharmacy chains may ask for evidence behind your claims. Wellness copy is easier to defend, but it also gives you less to defend with.
  • The lane is narrower in the EU and Japan than in the US. Copy that reads as clearly wellness to a US reviewer can look borderline to an EU one. Budget for separate copy per market.
  • Claim creep is real. The riskiest moment is not launch — it is month nine, when a growth marketer rewrites a bullet point to lift conversion. Governance, not intent, is what keeps you in the lane.

None of that argues against the wellness lane. It argues for choosing it deliberately, with the boundaries written down before the first purchase order rather than after the first listing takedown.

What do first-time importers ask most about wellness versus regulated classification?

First-time importers ask most about claims language, market differences, MOQ and who carries legal responsibility.

Is a heated neck massager a wellness product or a regulated device?

It is a wellness product if the intended use is comfort and relaxation, and it moves into a regulated pathway if the claims address a named condition. The hardware is identical either way; only the copy and the documentation change.

Does adding the word "therapy" to my product name make it regulated?

Not by itself. "Heat therapy" and "light therapy" are descriptive category terms. What moves a product is an outcome verb attached to a named condition, not the category noun.

Can I say my red light panel reduces wrinkles?

That phrasing makes a physiological promise and reads as a therapeutic claim. "Promotes the appearance of smoother-looking skin" keeps the product in the cosmetic-adjacent wellness lane in most markets.

What happens if I strengthen a claim after launch?

The classification changes retroactively, and the documentation you skipped becomes a gap you have to close under scrutiny. Retrofitting a regulated pathway onto a shipping product is significantly more expensive than starting there.

Do I need a special quality certification to sell a wellness product in the US or EU?

You need a competent manufacturing quality system, not a medical one. LIGHT BDB holds ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL-registered factory status and Sony Green Partner.

Is the classification the same in the US, EU and Japan?

The principle is the same everywhere — intended use drives classification — but the tolerance differs. The EU and Japan tend to read borderline claims more strictly than the US general wellness policy does, so plan separate copy decks.
