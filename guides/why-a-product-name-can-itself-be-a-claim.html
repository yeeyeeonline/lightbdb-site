What makes a product name a legal claim rather than just branding?

A product name is a legal claim when it asserts a function, benefit, or status that a regulator would require you to prove — and for wellness devices, that proof is usually a medical device clearance you do not have.

In the United States, the FDA regulates product labeling, and the name is part of the label. If your name contains "therapy," "medical," "clinical," "cure," or "treatment," the agency can classify your product as a medical device regardless of your intent. In the EU, the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) applies similar logic: a name that implies a medical purpose can force your wellness product into a regulatory pathway designed for Class I or higher devices. Japan's PMD Act takes the same position — the name is evidence of intended use.

This is not a theoretical risk. Marketplaces including Amazon, Walmart, and several EU platforms routinely delist products when the listing name implies medical function. The delisting is automated in many cases: a keyword scan flags the name, and the listing is suspended before a human reviews it. You lose the listing, your reviews, and your ranking — not because the product failed, but because the name made a claim you could not substantiate.

How regulators read your product name

  • FDA: The name is "labeling" under 21 CFR Part 801. A name that implies disease treatment triggers 510(k) or De Novo requirements.
  • EU MDR: Article 7 prohibits misleading claims. A name suggesting medical use reclassifies the product away from general wellness.
  • JP PMD Act: Intended use is inferred from the name, packaging, and instructions — the name alone can trigger pharmaceutical device classification.
  • Amazon: Listing names are scanned against a restricted-health-claims dictionary. "Therapy" and "clinical" are high-risk keywords.

Which words in a product name are the most dangerous for wellness devices?

The most dangerous words are "medical," "therapy," "clinical," "treatment," "cure," "heal," and any FDA abbreviation like "FDA" or "OTC" — each of these asserts a regulated status you must prove.

Industry-typical compliance guidance for wellness devices suggests avoiding these terms entirely, even in modified forms. "Ther-apy" with a hyphen, "Ther4py," or "TherapyPro" all read as the same claim to a regulator or an automated scanner. The same applies to "Med" as a prefix — "MedRelief" is a medical claim even though it is not the full word "medical."

There is a second tier of words that are not automatically medical but create risk in context. "Pain," "inflammation," "anxiety," "insomnia," and "depression" are condition names. A product name that pairs a condition with a benefit — "AnxietyEase Lamp" — implies treatment of a disease. A name that simply describes the light — "CalmGlow Lamp" — does not.

Safe naming patterns that still sell

  • Describe the physical action: "Heat," "Vibration," "Compression," "Light," "Pulse."
  • Describe the body area without a condition: "Neck," "Back," "Eye," "Shoulder."
  • Describe the sensation: "Warm," "Soothing," "Gentle," "Deep."
  • Describe the use context: "Home," "Travel," "Portable," "Daily."
  • Combine action + area + sensation: "WarmGlow Eye Massager" or "DeepHeat Back Pad."

How do you test whether your product name is safe before committing to production?

You test a product name by running it through three checks: a regulatory keyword scan, a marketplace search for similar names, and a plain-language read by someone who does not know your product.

First, the regulatory scan. Write your name and search for each word in the FDA's restricted claims list, the EU MDR's misleading claims provisions, and the JP PMD Act's intended-use guidance. If any word appears, the name is a claim. Second, search Amazon, Walmart, and the EU marketplaces for your exact name and close variants. If a competitor is selling a similar product under a similar name, check whether they have medical clearance — if they do and you do not, you are at a disadvantage. If they do not and they are still selling, they are either taking a risk you should not copy or they have a clearance you cannot see.

Third, the plain-language read. Give the name to someone outside your industry — a friend, a family member, a colleague in a different department. Ask them what the product does. If they say "it treats pain" or "it cures insomnia," your name is making a claim. If they say "it warms your neck" or "it gives a red glow," your name is descriptive and safe.

A practical naming checklist before you contact your ODM

  • Does the name contain "medical," "therapy," "clinical," "cure," "heal," or "treatment"? If yes, rewrite.
  • Does the name contain a condition name like "pain," "anxiety," or "insomnia"? If yes, rewrite.
  • Does the name contain "FDA," "OTC," or any regulatory abbreviation? If yes, rewrite immediately — this is the highest-risk category.
  • Does the name describe a physical action, a body area, or a sensation? If yes, it is likely safe.
  • Have you searched your name on Amazon and Google to check for existing products and potential trademark conflicts?

What are the honest trade-offs when you choose a safe product name?

The honest trade-off is that a safe, descriptive name is less emotionally evocative than a claim-based name, and it may require more marketing effort to communicate the benefit.

"PainEase Pro" tells a customer exactly what to expect in two words. "WarmGlow Massager" requires a subtitle, an image, or a review to convey the same benefit. That is a real cost. You are trading immediate comprehension for regulatory safety and listing longevity. For most wellness brands, that is the right trade — a delisted product sells nothing, and a name that survives for years compounds its value through reviews and rankings.

The second trade-off is that a safe name may feel generic in a crowded market. "Heat Pad" is safe but forgettable. The solution is not to move toward a medical claim but to add a distinctive brand element: "LumoHeat Pad" or "AuraWarm Neck Massager." The brand word carries the differentiation; the descriptive word carries the compliance.

There is also a cost consideration. If you insist on a name that borders on a claim, you may need to work with a factory that holds ISO 13485 and carries medical device registration. That factory will charge more, require longer lead times, and impose stricter documentation. Industry-typical cost increases for medical-grade manufacturing run 30–50% above wellness-grade manufacturing. LIGHT BDB does not offer that pathway — the company is explicit that it is a wellness-device ODM, not a medical device manufacturer. If you need a medical claim, you need a different partner. If you want a wellness product that sells for years without regulatory interruption, a descriptive name and a wellness-grade ODM is the faster, cheaper, and safer route.

Frequently asked questions about product names as claims

Can I name my wellness product "therapy" if it is not a medical device?

No. The word "therapy" implies a medical treatment, and regulators in the US, EU, and Japan treat the name as evidence of intended use. If your product is a wellness device without medical clearance, a name containing "therapy" can trigger regulatory action or marketplace delisting. Use a descriptive word like "warm," "soothe," or "relax" instead.

What happens if my product name gets flagged by Amazon or the FDA?

Amazon typically suspends the listing automatically when a name contains restricted health keywords. The FDA can issue a warning letter or request a recall if the name implies medical use without clearance. In both cases, you lose the listing, your reviews, and your sales momentum. The fix requires renaming the product and re-listing from scratch.
