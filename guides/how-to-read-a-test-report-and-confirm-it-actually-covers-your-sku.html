What should a test report actually prove for a wellness device?

A test report must prove your exact product meets the safety and performance standard you and your retailer require — not that a similar product from the same factory passed. Read it as evidence, not decoration, and check each required claim against your own specification.

For a wellness device such as a heated massager, red light therapy panel, or mood lamp, the report should cover four areas. First, electrical safety: insulation, leakage current, and heating behavior under normal operation. Second, electromagnetic compatibility (EMC), which many destination markets require even for wellness electronics. Third, battery safety if your product is rechargeable, including charge and discharge behavior. Fourth, material content such as heavy metals and restricted substances, which matters for EU and US retailers with sustainability policies.

Standards are destination-dependent. Most factories in this category test household electrical products against the IEC 60335 series, while US buyers often request the corresponding regional standard with a nationally recognized testing laboratory. Your retailer or compliance consultant will name the exact standard; the report must list that standard by number and edition.

A report that only says "passed" without a standard number, a description of test conditions, or a lab identity is a red flag. A useful report gives you enough detail to defend the product in a retailer compliance review or a customs inspection.

What the report does not prove

A test report does not prove that every unit in your container behaves identically to the sample. It certifies the specific sample submitted, manufactured under stated conditions. Production quality depends on the factory's process controls, which is why factory-level certifications matter as a complement to product-level testing.

How do I confirm the report covers my exact SKU and not a similar one?

Match four identifiers: model number, model name, brand (or blank ODM brand), and serial or batch reference, then confirm the photos and electrical ratings match your spec. If any identifier differs, the report belongs to a different SKU, even if the factory says the product is "the same."

Use this checklist when a factory sends you a report:

  • Model number matches your purchase order and the product label exactly.
  • Model name appears on the report cover or sample description page.
  • Serial or batch range covers your production window, not just a pre-production sample.
  • Photos show the same housing, cable, plug type, and control layout as your SKU.
  • Electrical ratings (input voltage, wattage, frequency) match your specification sheet.
  • Accessories tested (adapters, remotes, carrying pouches) match what ships in your retail box.

A factory may share a "family report" that covers several models tested under one representative model. This is legitimate when the standard allows family coverage and the representative model is listed. Confirm the representative model is clearly named and that your model falls within the stated scope. If the family scope is vague, ask the factory to share the lab's scope letter.

Ask also for the report language. A report in the destination country's language or in English with a translation is easier to present to customs and retailers. Many Chinese factories have English reports already on file.

What does a professional test report look like page by page?

A credible report runs 10 to 40 pages and opens with a cover page that names the client, the tested model, the standard, and the laboratory accreditation number. Without that cover page, you cannot verify the report's origin or scope.

Here is the standard anatomy of a laboratory test report:

  1. Cover page: client name, manufacturer, tested model, standard number and edition, report number, date, and the laboratory's accreditation mark.
  2. Sample description: photographs and a physical description of the tested unit, including dimensions, weight, and electrical ratings.
  3. Test conditions: ambient temperature, humidity, and any special setup such as heat sinks or load resistors.
  4. Results table: each clause of the standard listed with a pass or fail verdict and the measured value.
  5. Photographs of the test setup: wiring, insulation, and thermal measurements where relevant.
  6. Conclusion: a summary statement confirming compliance with the named standard.
  7. Annexes: lab scope, accreditation certificate, and any disclaimers about sample-only results.

Skip a report that lacks a results table. A single-page letter claiming compliance without measured values gives you nothing to defend in a dispute. Also check the report date. Industry-typical validity for many product standards is around three years, but several retailers accept reports up to five years old if the product has not changed. If your last report is older than your product revision, ask for a fresh test.

Which labs and standards should I insist on?

Insist on an accredited third-party laboratory rather than an in-house factory test note, and demand the standard your destination market requires. Accreditation bodies vary by country, but a credible lab publishes its accreditation number and scope on the report.

Three things to verify about the laboratory:

  • Accreditation: the lab should hold accreditation from a recognized body and list the scope that covers the standard tested.
  • Independence: the lab should not be owned by the factory or the trading company; a separate legal entity reduces conflict of interest.
  • Recognition: check that the lab is recognized by the authorities or retailers in your destination market, since some markets only accept specific laboratories.

Factory-level certifications do not replace product testing. They tell you the plant runs disciplined processes. These certifications give you confidence in process consistency and environmental and social compliance, but a UL registered factory is not the same as a UL-listed product. What are the honest limits of any factory-provided test report?

Every test report covers the specific sample tested, not the whole production batch, and certification becomes invalid if the product changes after the test. That is true for every factory in this category, including LIGHT BDB.

Be realistic about what a report cannot promise:

  • Batch variance: components, assembly line, and even weather can shift electrical performance across production runs. A sample that passes testing does not guarantee every container clears customs.
  • Report age: a report is a point-in-time statement. Design changes to a cable, battery, or heating element invalidate the original coverage.
  • Laboratory reputation: a report from an obscure lab may be rejected by customs or retailers even if the data is sound. Confirm recognition before paying.
  • Scope limits: wear, tear, and long-term durability are rarely covered by the product safety standards applied to wellness devices. Those behaviors live outside the report.
  • Factory certification nuance: a UL registered factory is a process registration, not a product listing. Do not market your product as certified based on the factory's registration alone.

The fix is simple: treat the report as one input, pair it with a factory audit of your own, and keep the report tied to a specific sample you have physically inspected. That combination gives your private-label brand a defensible foundation.
