What a Washable Heating Assembly Requires in Sealing

Quick answer

A washable heating assembly needs three sealing layers working together: a compression gasket matched to the wash method, a potted or overmolded electronics chamber, and a pressure-equalizing vent — all validated across 20–50 wash cycles rather than a single splash test.

  • IPX7 means 1 m immersion for 30 minutes; it is the usual floor for a hand-rinseable heated product.
  • Industry-typical validation runs 20–50 wash-and-dry cycles with detergent before a sealed design is signed off.
  • Silicone gaskets need roughly 20–30% compression; below that, heating cycles gradually open the seam.
  • Potting the electronics chamber in two-part polyurethane or epoxy is the lowest-cost route to surviving immersion.
  • An unmolded cable exit is the most common leak path in heated wellness devices.
  • An ePTFE vent equalizes internal pressure during heat-up and cool-down so the seals are not pumped open.

What does "washable" actually mean for a heated assembly — is IPX7 enough?

IPX7 is the minimum most buyers should specify for a hand-washable heating assembly, and on its own it is still an incomplete specification.

The IP code in IEC 60529 describes water ingress on a rising scale. IPX4 handles splashing water from any direction, IPX5 handles a directed low-pressure jet, IPX6 handles powerful jets, and IPX7 handles immersion at 1 metre for 30 minutes. IPX8 is defined by agreement between buyer and factory and is normally deeper or longer than IPX7.

None of those tests include detergent, mechanical agitation, or the thermal cycling that a heated product puts its own seals through. That gap is where most "washable" claims quietly break down.

Practical buyers usually land on one of four tiers:

  • Wipe-clean only (IPX0–IPX2): a damp cloth over the surface. You need splash protection at the seam and nothing more.
  • Shower and splash (IPX4–IPX5): heated massagers used in a wet bathroom. A seam gasket, sealed button membrane and a port door cover it.
  • Rinse under a tap (IPX6–IPX7): the tier most "washable" marketing actually sits in. It needs potting plus a gasket plus a vent.
  • Machine wash: no IP test covers drum agitation. It is validated by an internal protocol, industry-typically 20–50 cycles with detergent, rinse and dry.

Be precise in your own spec. "Washable" without a number invites a return, because the customer's definition of washing is always more aggressive than the engineer's. Write the wash method into the product requirement document, then design the seal around that method and no more.

Which sealing method should a heated assembly use — gasket, ultrasonic weld, overmold, or potting?

Most washable heated assemblies need two sealing methods layered, not one: a compression gasket on every serviceable seam, and potting or overmolding around the electronics.

The methods are not interchangeable. Each one buys you a different combination of protection, tooling cost and repairability, so the choice usually follows your volume and your service model rather than the seal itself.

Compression gaskets

A molded silicone gasket is the workhorse of the category. Industry practice targets a gland fill ratio of roughly 15–30% and a squeeze of 20–30% of the gasket section height. Below that range the gasket never reaches full contact; above it, the material takes a permanent set and stops recovering after a few heat cycles.

Specify low-compression-set silicone (VMQ) in the 30–50 Shore A range, and put compression limiters at every screw boss so assembly torque cannot crush the gasket flat. Gaskets are the only mainstream method that lets the user or your service partner open the product without destroying it.

Ultrasonic and laser welding

Welding two resin halves produces a homogenous, glue-free joint and is the highest-volume approach once the shell geometry is stable. It is unforgiving of resin variation, moisture in the pellets and uneven wall thickness, and it is destructive — there is no rework path.

Overmolding

A TPE or TPU layer molded directly over a PC/ABS shell creates a chemical bond at the interface rather than a compression fit. It is the best answer for cable exits, grips and button surrounds, because the material flows around the feature instead of squeezing against it.

Potting and encapsulation

Two-part polyurethane or epoxy fills the electronics chamber and leaves no air pocket for water to occupy. Polyurethane stays flexible and suits assemblies with moving wiring; epoxy is harder and more dimensionally stable. Both make the board permanently unserviceable, which is a warranty decision as much as an engineering one.

Where do washable heated assemblies actually leak?

Most field leaks cluster at five features: the cable exit, button membranes, lens seams, screw bosses and the wire itself.

Teardown reports across the category repeat the same list, and none of them are exotic:

  1. The cable exit. A cable passing through a plain hole with a rubber grommet will leak within a few dozen flex cycles. The jacket moves, the grommet does not, and a gap opens.
  2. Wicking along stranded conductors. Water travels inside the copper strands by capillary action, straight past a perfectly good outer seal and into the electronics chamber. Sealed crimps and a potted splice stop it.
  3. Button and switch membranes. Membrane switches bond to the shell with adhesive. Detergent and warm water attack that adhesive edge first.
  4. Lens and light-guide seams. Light and mood products often have a decorative window bonded with tape. It is the weakest joint on the unit.
  5. Screw bosses and tolerance stack-up. Uneven clamp load around a gasket leaves one side under-compressed. That side leaks.
  6. Gasket compression set. Heat accelerates it. A seal that passes the first wash can fail the twentieth.

If you are reviewing a design, ask to see the gasket groove dimensions, the boss compression limiters and the cable transition in cross-section. Three drawings will tell you more than a datasheet claim.

How do you seal the power cable and connector on a washable heated product?

Seal the cable exit with a molded strain relief that bonds to the jacket, and remove the charging port entirely if the product can charge wirelessly.

The cable is the hardest feature to seal because it is the only part of the assembly that moves. A proper exit is overmolded or molded directly onto the jacket, so the seal is a material bond rather than a compression fit. Behind it, the conductors should terminate in a sealed crimp or a hot-melt potted splice, so that even if water enters the jacket it stops before the PCB.

Connectors follow the same logic. A USB-C port on a washable device needs a gasketed door that the user will actually close, or a recessed pogo-pin interface with gold-plated contacts and a hydrophobic seal behind them. Users leave doors open. Design for that behaviour rather than assuming compliance.

Wireless charging sidesteps the problem completely: no opening, no door, no seal to age. How do you handle condensation and pressure cycling inside a sealed heated shell?

Fit a breathable ePTFE vent so the shell can equalize pressure without letting liquid water in.

A heated product is a small pressure vessel. Air inside expands as the element warms and contracts as it cools, and every cycle pushes and pulls on the seals. On cool-down the internal pressure drops below ambient, which actively draws water and vapour toward any imperfect joint. Sealing the shell harder does not fix this; it makes the pressure differential worse.

A hydrophobic membrane vent solves it. Supplier data typically quote water entry pressures around 0.5 bar and above, while air passes freely. Place the vent away from the heating element, orient it so it cannot sit in a puddle during washing, and protect it from detergent film with a small baffle.

  • Do not substitute desiccant for a vent. It saturates and then becomes a water reservoir.
  • Assemble in a low-humidity room; trapping humid air inside a sealed shell guarantees condensation on the first cold morning.
  • Choose vent adhesive that survives the detergents and oils the product will actually meet, including sunscreen and body lotion.

What testing actually proves a heating assembly survives washing?

Run a repeated wash cycle, not a single immersion: detergent wash, rinse, dry, then verify electrical integrity — and repeat it many times.

A one-off IPX7 dunk tells you the seal worked once, on a prototype, on the day it was built. It tells you very little about the unit that ships in month nine.

An industry-typical validation protocol looks like this:

  • 20–50 full cycles, each with detergent at warm wash temperature, a clean rinse, and a controlled dry.
  • Function and insulation-resistance checks after each block of cycles, not only at the end.
  • Dye penetrant inspection on a sample of units after cycling to locate weeps that electrical tests miss.
  • A combined thermal-and-wash sequence, because heat is what ages the gasket.
  • Drop testing after washing, since a wet unit that is dropped loads its seals differently.
  • Chemical swabs with sunscreen, body oil and alcohol-based cleaners on the gasket and lens bonds.

What does sealing add to tooling cost, MOQ and lead time?

Sealing adds tooling steps and validation rounds, so expect a higher MOQ and a longer first-article timeline than an unsealed product.

The delta is real. A gasket groove, a second-shot overmold or a potting fixture each add tool work, and a welded shell adds a weld nest plus a fixture for leak testing. Industry-typically, sealing-related tooling adds a meaningful step change over a plain enclosure tool, and machine-wash validation adds at least one extra build-and-test round before mass production.

What are the honest trade-offs of a fully sealed, washable design?

Sealing costs money, adds assembly time and usually makes repair impossible — that trade is worth it only when washing is central to your product story.

Three trade-offs deserve a straight answer up front.

Repairability disappears. A potted, welded shell cannot be opened without destroying it. Every field failure becomes a replacement instead of a repair, and that has to be priced into your returns model. LIGHT BDB's 500-day limited warranty is one of the longest in the category, which supports that model — but it does not make a sealed unit serviceable.

Over-specifying costs money with no user benefit. If the product lives on a nightstand and is wiped with a cloth, IPX7 tooling is spend you will never recover. Match the seal to the documented use case, then publish exactly that rating.

Seals age, and vents can clog. Gasket compression set, adhesive creep and detergent film on a membrane vent are slow failure modes that appear in month twelve, not month one. Build the ageing test into your validation rather than discovering it through warranty claims.

The balanced position: seal decisively where water actually reaches — cable exits, buttons, lenses, electronics — and avoid sealing the whole product if the use case does not require it. That is a design and cost conversation worth having at the drawing stage, not after tooling is cut.
