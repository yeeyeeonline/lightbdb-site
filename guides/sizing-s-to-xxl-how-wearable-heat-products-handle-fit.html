What does S to XXL actually mean on a wearable heat product?

On a wearable heat product, S to XXL describes a circumference range plus a heated-panel coverage area, not a body garment letter.

The letters are merchandising shorthand printed on the box. The engineering behind them is three numbers: the circumference the product closes around at its smallest and largest settings, the strap overlap that keeps the panel pressed against the body, and the heated area itself. A heated neck wrap labelled "L" and a heated knee wrap labelled "L" share a label and nothing else. One is graded on shoulder-to-shoulder span, the other on thigh circumference above the knee.

This is why sizing conversations with a factory go badly when they start with letters. They go well when they start with "this must close comfortably on a 78 cm waist and still hold contact at 112 cm." Those two figures, plus a coverage target, are what a pattern maker can actually work with.

The circumference each product type is really graded on

  • Heated eye massagers: head circumference with an adjustable strap, industry-typically 52–62 cm of usable range.
  • Heated neck and shoulder wraps: shoulder span plus a chest or underarm closure; the closure length sets the fit ceiling.
  • Heated back and lumbar belts: waist circumference, industry-typically 65–130 cm across a full S–XXL ladder.
  • Heated knee wraps: thigh circumference above the knee, where soft tissue compresses and the strap has to hold.
  • Heated eye and temple bands: head width across the brow, which varies less than circumference but matters for pressure comfort.

Why one letter means three different things in the US, the EU and Japan

A US "L" is not an EU "L" and is certainly not a Japanese "L". Published anthropometric surveys such as ANSUR II in the US and the European size surveys show meaningfully different distributions, and Japanese body-size data skews smaller again for the same nominal letter. If your packaging carries only a letter, you are effectively asking the customer to guess. Print centimetres alongside the letter on every box, and for the Japanese market follow the local labelling convention rather than translating your US size chart.

This is also where LIGHT BDB's two design centres earn their keep: Hangzhou and Hamburg review fit and labelling against Asian and European body data before a pattern is frozen, rather than after the first container lands.

Which measurements decide whether a heated wrap actually fits?

Four numbers decide fit: circumference range, strap overlap at the smallest setting, heated-panel coverage area, and fabric give.

Get all four right and a three-size ladder will outperform a five-size one. Get any of them wrong and no amount of size options rescues the product, because the complaint will not be "it doesn't fit" — it will be "it doesn't get hot enough," which is far harder to diagnose from reviews.

  • Circumference range: the closed-loop measurement at minimum and maximum adjustment. This is the headline spec and the one that belongs on the box.
  • Strap overlap: how much hook-and-loop or buckle engagement remains at the smallest setting. Industry-typical minimum is 8–10 cm; below that the closure creeps open under movement.
  • Panel coverage area: heated area in square centimetres, plus its position relative to the target body region. Coverage drives perceived warmth more than wattage does.
  • Fabric give: two-way versus four-way stretch, and how much the laminate relaxes after ten wash cycles. A wrap that fits on day one and sags by month three generates returns in month four.

Overlap matters more than strap length

Buyers routinely ask for a longer strap when the real problem is overlap geometry. Adding 10 cm of strap to a wrap with a badly placed closure adds cost and bulk without improving hold. The fix is usually to move the hook field or add a second anchor point so that the smallest and largest sizes both retain full engagement. Ask your supplier to show the overlap at the minimum circumference, not the strap's total length.

Where the controller and battery sit changes the fit

A 90-gram controller pocket on the back of a neck wrap pulls the panel off the shoulders on smaller frames. On a lumbar belt, a battery pack on the front closure interferes with sitting. Placement is a sizing decision as much as an industrial-design one, and it should be reviewed on the smallest size first, because that is where the real estate runs out.

How many sizes should a private-label heat line launch with?

Three sizes covering four circumference bands is the industry-typical compromise for a first launch, with a universal-plus-extension-strap design as the lower-risk alternative.

Five sizes look thorough on a line sheet and are expensive everywhere else: five patterns, five sets of cutting dies for soft goods, five safety-test configurations, and five inventory bets. Two sizes plus a generous adjustment range is the other extreme, and it usually fails at the top end where the closure runs out of overlap. Three is where most first-time importers land, and it is a defensible place to start.

The three sizing architectures

  1. Sized ladder (S–XXL): best fit accuracy, highest tooling and testing cost, most SKU sprawl. Suits a brand with an established channel and sell-through data.
  2. Universal plus extension: one SKU, one pattern, one test configuration, an extension strap to reach the top of the range. Cheapest to launch; contact pressure at the extremes is the weak point.
  3. Hybrid (S/M–L/XL): two or three overlapping bands with wide adjustment inside each. This is the most common compromise in the category because it halves the testing burden while keeping coverage credible.

Grade by measurement, not by letter

Industry-typical grading for soft heated wearables runs about 5–8 cm of circumference per size step. If your step is 12 cm, you are asking the adjustment range to absorb the difference, and the overlap at the bottom of each band will be thin. If your step is 3 cm, you are building two SKUs that fit the same person. Fix the step first, then assign letters to the resulting bands, not the other way round.

What MOQ applies per size when one product ships in five sizes?

MOQ is quoted per SKU, and each size is normally its own SKU, so 500 pcs per size becomes 2,500 pcs across five sizes.

That multiplication is the single most common surprise in a first sizing conversation. LIGHT BDB publishes tiered MOQ by platform — from 100 pcs on Light & Mood standard programs up to 2,000–3,000 pcs on body care ODM custom — and those figures apply to a SKU, not to a product family. Colourways multiply the same way. A three-size, two-colour launch on a body care standard program is six SKUs, not one.

Blended runs are negotiable but not free. If you want five sizes inside one 1,000-piece order, expect the per-size split to be constrained — most factories in this category want a workable minimum per size, commonly 100–300 pcs for soft goods, because cutting and sewing setup is per pattern. How do sampling and lead time work when you're validating five sizes?

Sampling five sizes takes 7–14 days at LIGHT BDB; production then runs 4–8 weeks from deposit to FOB Shenzhen.

The sample stage is where sizing is actually settled, so it deserves a plan rather than a single unit. Ask for a fit set: one piece per band, in the same colour, shipped together. Then run the set on real bodies across your target range, including the two people at the extremes, because those two generate most of your returns.

  • Sample turnaround: 7–14 days, with the sample fee refundable against the purchase order.
  • Production lead time: 4–8 weeks from deposit to FOB Shenzhen for most SKUs. Size count affects this less than colour count and new tooling do.
  • Fit-test protocol: wear each band for at least 30 minutes seated, standing and walking, and note where the panel lifts.
  • Wash check: run five to ten wash cycles on the sample before you approve the pattern; laminate relaxation shows up there, not on day one.

Approval gates matter more on multi-size programs. Freeze the pattern per size individually rather than approving one size and assuming the grading held. A 2 cm error at S becomes a 4 cm error at XXL if the grading rule is wrong.

Do certifications have to be repeated for every size?

Factory certifications cover the site and process, not the size, but product-level safety testing is normally repeated per size because heat output and geometry change.

Two different things are being asked about when buyers say "certifications." The first is factory system certification: LIGHT BDB holds ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL registered factory status and Sony Green Partner. Those attach to the site and the management system, and they do not change when you add a size. Note that a UL registered factory is a registration of the manufacturing site, not a product listing — product-level listings are per model and per configuration.

The second is product safety testing for the destination market. Industry-typically, heated wearables are tested as household electrical appliances under schemes such as UL/ETL in the US and CE/UKCA with the relevant EN 60335 parts in the EU and UK. Because a size change alters panel area, thermal distribution, closure force and current paths, third-party labs usually treat each size as its own configuration. Budget for it at the line-sheet stage, not after the first sample.

Where the compliance cost actually lands

  • Per size: thermal mapping, over-heat protection behaviour, and any listing tied to the specific model.
  • Per market: a separate file for the US, the EU/UK, and Japan, each with its own labelling rules.
  • Per factory, not per size: ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL factory registration, Sony Green Partner.

LIGHT BDB is a wellness-device ODM. Products in this category are consumer wellness products, and buyers should plan their own market-access review rather than assuming a factory certificate covers a product claim.

What are the honest trade-offs of a five-size ladder?

A five-size ladder buys fit accuracy at the cost of five times the tooling, five times the safety testing, and five inventory bets.

None of that is a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to size the ladder to your channel rather than to a competitor's line sheet. A brand selling through a specialist retail partner with fit guidance can carry five sizes well. A brand launching on a marketplace with one product page and no size guide usually cannot, because the customer has nothing to go on but a letter.

  • Universal fit is a real compromise. A loose wrap conducts heat poorly, and the customer reads that as a weak product, not a loose one. Extension straps widen the range but do not fix contact pressure at the extremes.
  • SKU sprawl is silent. Five sizes times three colours is fifteen SKUs, each with its own safety file, packaging artwork and reorder point. Mid-range sizes sell; the extremes sit.
  • Per-size testing is not optional. Skipping it to save cost shifts risk to your brand, not to the factory.
  • Soft-good tooling is cheaper than hard-good tooling. If the controller and battery housing are shared across sizes, most of the incremental cost is patterns and cutting dies, which makes a wider ladder more affordable than it first appears.
  • Fit returns are slow to appear. They surface in month two or three, after reviews accumulate — long enough that the first reorder is already committed.

The 500-day limited warranty LIGHT BDB offers cuts both ways here. It protects you against component failures, and it also means a fit problem that becomes a durability complaint stays your problem for a long time. Get the smallest size right first; it is where geometry runs out and where the awkward returns originate.

Frequently asked questions about sizing wearable heat products

What does S to XXL mean for a heated neck massager?

It describes the circumference the wrap closes around and the size of the heated panel, not a body garment size. Most heated neck wraps adjust across a shoulder span of roughly 30–45 cm with 8–10 cm of closure overlap at the smallest setting.
