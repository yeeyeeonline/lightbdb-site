What before and after imagery implies to a regulator

Quick answer

Before and after imagery in wellness-device marketing signals to a regulator that you are making a performance claim, which triggers substantiation requirements under FTC, FDA, and EU consumer-protection rules. You need competent and reliable evidence, typically from a controlled study, before publishing any comparative visual.   • Regulators require "competent and reliable scientific evidence" for any health-related performance claim.

  • EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive requires proof for claims made to consumers.

Why do regulators care about before and after imagery at all?

Regulators care because before and after imagery is an express performance claim, and every performance claim must be substantiated before it reaches the consumer.

When you publish a side-by-side comparison, you are not showing a photo. You are asserting that your product caused the visible change. That assertion falls squarely within advertising law in the US, EU, and Japan. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) in the US, the European Commission via the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, and Japan's Consumer Affairs Agency all treat visual claims as binding representations about what a product does.

For wellness devices specifically, the risk is elevated because the category sits near the medical boundary. A red light therapy panel that shows skin improvement in a before/after image is making a physiological claim. A heated massager that shows reduced swelling is making a physiological claim. Regulators do not need to prove you intended to make a medical claim — they only need to show that a reasonable consumer would interpret the imagery as a promise of results.

What counts as a claim in the eyes of a regulator

  • A photo pair showing skin texture change implies a cosmetic or dermatological effect.
  • A photo pair showing posture or mobility change implies a musculoskeletal effect.
  • A photo pair showing mood or energy change implies a neurological or psychological effect.
  • Even implied timelines (e.g., "week 1" vs "week 6") create a duration claim.
  • Text captions on the imagery are treated as part of the claim, not separate from it.

What evidence do regulators expect before you publish before and after imagery?

Regulators expect competent and reliable scientific evidence, which in practice means a controlled study with a defined methodology, measurable endpoints, and statistical analysis.

The FTC's standard for health-related claims is the most frequently cited benchmark. It requires "competent and reliable scientific evidence" — defined as tests, analyses, research, or studies that have been conducted and evaluated in an objective manner by qualified persons, using procedures generally accepted in the profession to yield accurate and reliable results. For most wellness-device claims, that means a human clinical study, not a lab test and not customer testimonials.

The EU applies a similar standard under the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, which prohibits misleading actions and omissions. If you show a before/after image, you must be able to prove the claim is true. The burden of proof sits with the advertiser, not the regulator.

Typical substantiation requirements by claim type

  • Cosmetic appearance claims: Industry-typical substantiation involves a controlled study with 30–60 subjects per group, blinded evaluators, and standardized photography.
  • Comfort or relaxation claims: Regulators generally accept subjective outcome measures, but still expect a defined protocol and a statistically significant result.
  • Performance claims (e.g., "reduces pain"): These approach medical territory and require the highest level of evidence — randomized controlled trials are industry-typical.
  • Duration claims (e.g., "results last 8 hours"): You need time-series data from a study that measures the endpoint at the claimed time point.

What happens if you publish before and after imagery without substantiation?

If you publish before and after imagery without substantiation, you risk an enforcement action that can include fines, corrective advertising, refunds, and a consent decree restricting future marketing.

The FTC has pursued wellness and beauty brands for unsubstantiated before/after claims repeatedly. Typical outcomes include monetary penalties, mandatory consumer refunds, and a requirement to run corrective messaging. In the EU, national consumer-protection authorities can order the removal of advertising and impose fines per day of continued non-compliance. Japan's Consumer Affairs Agency can issue cease-and-desist orders and publish the offending company's name, which carries significant reputational damage in the Japanese market.

Realistic risk scenarios for a private-label brand

  • A competitor files a complaint with the FTC or a national consumer authority, triggering an investigation.
  • A consumer posts a negative review showing results that do not match your imagery, which becomes evidence of deception.
  • A retailer (Amazon, Walmart, or a European marketplace) requests substantiation documents before allowing your listing to go live.
  • An influencer who posted your imagery is held jointly liable, which can escalate the dispute.

How should a brand owner approach before and after imagery for a wellness device?

A brand owner should treat before and after imagery as a high-risk marketing asset that requires either solid substantiation or a deliberate reframing away from physiological claims.

There are three defensible paths. First, commission a proper study through a qualified third-party lab or contract research organization. Second, shift the visual narrative away from body changes and toward experiential outcomes — showing the device in use, the light output, the heat distribution, or the comfort of the form factor. Third, use imagery that documents objective product attributes rather than user outcomes, such as a thermal camera showing heat coverage or a spectroradiometer reading showing light output.

What are the honest trade-offs when using before and after imagery in this category?

The honest trade-off is that before and after imagery is the most persuasive format in wellness marketing, but it is also the most likely to trigger regulatory scrutiny, and the cost of substantiation often exceeds the marketing budget of a first-time importer.

Commissioning a defensible study for a physiological claim can cost tens of thousands of dollars and take months. For a brand launching a private-label device at a 100–3,000 unit MOQ, that cost is rarely justified by the expected revenue. The alternative — using attribute-based imagery that shows the device's output rather than the user's body — is less emotionally compelling but far safer.

There is also a reputational risk that is harder to quantify. Even if you never face an enforcement action, consumers increasingly scrutinize before/after imagery. A brand that publishes unsubstantiated comparisons invites negative reviews and social-media pushback that can damage the product before it gains traction.
