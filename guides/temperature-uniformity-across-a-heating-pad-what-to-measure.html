Temperature Uniformity Across a Heating Pad: What to Measure

Quick answer

Temperature uniformity is the spread between the hottest and coldest point on a heating pad surface at steady state — the ΔT figure. Measure it with a 9-point grid after a 30–60 minute soak in still air at 23±2°C, then report max, min, mean and the location of each extreme.

  • Uniformity is a ΔT figure: hottest minus coldest surface point at steady state.
  • A 3x3 grid is the minimum defensible layout; 15–25 points resolve edge gradients properly.
  • Define steady state as drift under 0.5°C across 10 minutes, usually reached in 30–60 minutes.
  • Hold ambient at 23±2°C with no forced airflow, or results are not comparable between labs.
  • Voltage drives output: a 120V pad run at 100V delivers roughly 69% of rated power.
  • Industry-typical well-designed pads hold ΔT within 2–3°C across the central zone.

What does temperature uniformity actually mean on a heating pad spec sheet?

It means the spread between the hottest and coldest point on the pad surface once the temperature has stopped climbing.

That spread is written as ΔT. If a pad settles at 42°C near the centre and 35°C in one corner, its ΔT is 7°C. Every other uniformity number — mean surface temperature, standard deviation, any "±" tolerance — is built on top of that one measurement, and every one of them is meaningless unless the test conditions are printed next to it.

ΔT is the number to quote first

ΔT answers the only question a user actually asks: is one part of this pad noticeably colder than another? As a working rule of thumb, consumers tend to notice a step of roughly 2°C or more on a warm contact surface, and anything under that reads as even. Report ΔT as an absolute figure in °C, always with the point map that produced it.

Mean, standard deviation and coefficient of variation

  • Mean surface temperature — the average of every grid point. This is what the user experiences as "how warm the pad is", not the number on the dial.
  • Standard deviation (SD) — how tightly the points cluster around that mean. A pad can show a low ΔT and a high SD if heat is patchy in many places rather than concentrated in one cold corner.
  • Coefficient of variation (CV) — SD divided by mean, as a percentage. CV lets you compare a 40°C pad with a 55°C pad fairly; industry-typical targets for well-controlled flexible heaters land in the low single digits.

The four different things "uniformity" can mean

Suppliers conflate these constantly, so ask which one is being quoted.

  1. Spatial uniformity — point-to-point spread at one moment in steady state. This is the classic ΔT.
  2. Temporal stability — how much a single point drifts over 30–60 minutes once hot.
  3. Setpoint accuracy — how close mean surface temperature sits to the controller setting.
  4. Warm-up uniformity — the spread during the ramp. A pad can be even at 45 minutes and badly uneven at 5.

Then watch the "±" symbol. A spec reading "uniformity ±2°C" can mean a total spread of 4°C (half-range convention) or a total spread of 2°C (max deviation from the mean). Most suppliers in this category quote half-range, which flatters the figure. Ask explicitly — it is the single most common misunderstanding in heating pad specifications.

How many measurement points do you need across a heating pad surface?

Nine points in a 3x3 grid is the minimum defensible layout; fifteen to twenty-five points is better for a pad larger than roughly 30 x 40 cm.

Nine points tell you whether a problem exists. They rarely tell you where it comes from, because the coldest zone on a heating pad is almost never in the middle of a grid cell — it usually sits at the boundary between the element and the seam.

Where the extra points must go

  • Over each end of the heating element, where the resistive trace terminates.
  • Over the cable entry and the controller connector.
  • Directly over the thermistor or thermostat that the controller reads.
  • 1 cm inside the seam, and again at 3 cm, to map the edge gradient rather than sample it.
  • Anywhere the cover is double-layered, quilted or heat-sealed.
  • On a folding line, if the pad is designed to fold for storage.

Decide the quoted area before the test, not after

Every flexible heating pad has an edge band, usually 3–5 cm, that runs colder than the usable area. Testing the full outline always produces a worse ΔT than testing the inset area. Neither is dishonest, but the report has to say which applies. Ask: "Is the ΔT quoted over the full pad outline or an inset usable area, and what is the inset?"

Grid density is a cost decision

More points means more thermocouples, more logger channels and more mounting time. For a first validation run, 9–15 points is enough to make a decision. For a production qualification report you intend to hand a retailer, 25 points and a printed point map is a better answer.

What test conditions and instruments produce a repeatable uniformity reading?

Contact thermocouples on a fixed grid, a steady 23±2°C ambient with no forced airflow, and a defined test bed give the only readings you can compare between labs.

Thermocouples for numbers, thermal imaging for diagnosis

  • Contact thermocouples (T or K type) are the reporting instrument. Typical accuracy is ±0.5°C, and thin leads (around 0.1 mm) reduce heat conducted away from the bead.
  • Mounting matters more than the sensor. Kapton tape plus a thin thermally conductive adhesive, or a small loaded pad, gives more repeatable results than tape alone — contact pressure changes the reading.
  • Infrared cameras find a hotspot in ten seconds and report it badly. Fabric and foam emissivity typically sits around 0.85–0.95 (industry-typical); if the camera is set to 0.95 and the cover is 0.88, the reading can be off by several degrees.

Ambient, loading and dwell time

  1. Ambient: 23±2°C, still air, out of sunlight and away from draughts. A pad tested next to an air-conditioning vent will always look less uniform.
  2. Loading: test the way the product is used. Most safety standards for flexible heating appliances, including IEC 60335-2-17, test on a defined test bed rather than in free air — and free-air testing flatters uniformity because there is no compression and no insulation on top.
  3. Dwell: run 30–60 minutes and stop when drift is under 0.5°C across a 10-minute window. Report the window you used.

Voltage control and repeat runs

Feed the pad from a regulated supply set to nominal (120V, 230V or 100V), log at 1 Hz minimum with 0.1°C resolution, then repeat at ±10% of nominal. Finally, repeat the whole run three times, reseating sensors each time. If ΔT moves more than about 1°C between identical runs, your method is the variable — fix that before you judge the product.

A seven-step setup checklist

  1. Condition the unpowered pad 30 minutes at 23±2°C.
  2. Mark the point map on the cover before mounting anything.
  3. Mount sensors with consistent, documented contact pressure.
  4. Power at nominal voltage and log for 60 minutes.
  5. Confirm steady state: under 0.5°C drift over 10 minutes.
  6. Compute max, min, mean, ΔT and SD for the steady-state window.
  7. Photograph the sensor map and attach it to the report.

Which numbers belong in a heating pad uniformity test report?

A usable report names the test standard, ambient, voltage, dwell time, point map, sample size, and the max, min, mean and ΔT for every unit tested.

The minimum contents

  • Test method or standard referenced, plus the test date.
  • Ambient temperature and airflow condition.
  • Supply voltage, and whether ±10% runs were included.
  • Test bed and covering used.
  • Dwell time and steady-state criterion.
  • Point map with sensor count and coordinates.
  • Quoted area (full pad or inset).
  • Per-unit max, min, mean, ΔT and SD.
  • Unit serial or lot numbers.
  • Location of the hottest and coldest point on each unit.

Sample size and lot coverage

Three units is a working minimum, and they should come from three different production lots — not three units pulled from the same bench batch. A single hand-built golden sample tells you what the design can do; it tells you nothing about what a 3,000-piece order will do. For ongoing supply, ask for a defined re-test frequency rather than a one-off certificate.

Five questions to put in writing

  1. Which method or standard did you test against?
  2. Is the ΔT quoted over the full pad outline or an inset usable area?
  3. Is the "±" figure half-range or full-range?
  4. How many units, from how many lots, and can we see the serial numbers?
  5. Can we get raw per-point data and the sensor map, not just a summary line?

Why do heating pad edges and corners run colder than the center?

Because the resistive trace ends there — the last few centimetres receive no Joule heating while still losing heat to the air and the cover.

Four effects stack up at the perimeter:

  • Element termination. The trace has to stop somewhere. At the end, current density drops, and the termination hardware — bus bar, crimp or stitched connection — does not dissipate heat the same way as the trace.
  • Perimeter loss. A centre point loses heat sideways in every direction and upward once. A corner point loses heat on three sides. Even with identical heat input, the corner runs cooler.
  • Cover geometry. Seams, quilting, binding tape and heat-sealed edges add layers between element and surface. More insulation in between means a colder top surface.
  • Controller interference. The cable entry and controller box create either a cold shadow or a local warm patch, depending on how the electronics dissipate heat and how the wiring routes.

Where the sensor sits changes everything

If the thermistor sits at the hottest point on the pad, the controller holds that point at target and lets the average surface run colder. If it sits near an edge, the centre can overshoot before the sensor reacts. Sensor placement is a uniformity decision, not just a control decision, and it belongs in the test report next to the measured surface map.

Folding and bunching behaviour

Most safety standards for flexible heating appliances include a folded, bunched or rolled test condition, because folded fabric traps heat and can spike local surface temperature well above the flat-pad reading. Uniformity data measured flat does not predict what happens when a user folds the pad over a chair back or bunches it under a cushion. If your product is likely to be folded in use, request a folded-condition temperature map as a separate deliverable.

How does supply voltage change uniformity and setpoint accuracy?

Power scales with the square of voltage, so a pad rated at 120V run at 100V delivers about 69% of its rated heat output.

The maths is P = V²/R. At 100V instead of 120V, output is (100/120)² ≈ 0.69. At 132V instead of 120V, it is roughly 1.21. One relationship explains most cross-market uniformity complaints.

Nominal voltages differ by market

  • US and Canada: 120V, with ±10% variation putting real-world supply between roughly 108V and 132V.
  • EU and UK: 230V nominal, again with ±10% tolerance.
  • Japan: 100V in most regions, with 200V in parts of the country.

A resistive element designed for 120V will run cooler and take longer to reach setpoint on a 100V supply. That is physics, not a defect. If you sell one SKU into all three markets, the product needs either a voltage-aware controller or separate market builds.

What control strategy does to uniformity

  • Simple thermostat with fixed duty cycle: cheapest, and the most sensitive to line voltage. Expect the widest ΔT.
  • Electronic controller with voltage sensing and duty-cycle control: holds effective power closer to target across markets, which usually tightens both setpoint accuracy and uniformity.
  • Multi-zone element design: separate traces with proportionally weighted control. The most effective way to close the edge gap, and the most expensive.

One useful rule of thumb: lower effective power generally improves spatial uniformity, because smaller gradients build up, while hurting setpoint accuracy and warm-up time. That is why a pad tested at 100V can look more even than the same pad at 132V.

What are the honest trade-offs and limits of uniformity testing?

Tighter uniformity costs money and time, and no test on three samples proves what a 3,000-unit run will do.

Five limits worth naming plainly:

  1. A tight spec across the full outline, corners included, is genuinely hard. Specifying the central usable area and accepting 5–8°C at the extreme perimeter is what most of the category does. Ask for both numbers instead of forcing one figure to cover everything.
  2. Uniformity fights warm-up speed. Denser element patterns and lower watt density even out the surface and take longer to reach temperature. Request the warm-up curve alongside the steady-state map.
  3. A real test takes real lab time. Sixty-minute soak, three repeats, three units, two voltage conditions is roughly a full working day per SKU — an industry-typical figure, not a LIGHT BDB quotation. A supplier reporting surface uniformity after ten minutes is usually reporting the sensor, not the surface.
  4. Thermal images are not measurements. Persuasive, and routinely wrong by several degrees on fabric surfaces. Use them to find problems; use thermocouples to report numbers.
  5. One report is a snapshot, not a system. The number that matters is the one your supplier can reproduce next quarter, on a different lot, without you asking.
