Speaker Lamp OEM: What Bluetooth Plus Light Means for Certification

Quick answer

A Bluetooth speaker lamp needs three approval stacks, not one: radio (FCC ID in the US, CE-RED in the EU, MIC giteki in Japan), electrical and photobiological safety for the light, and Bluetooth SIG qualification. Plan 3–6 weeks of testing per market after design freeze, plus a low-to-mid four-figure USD fee per model.

  • Bluetooth makes your lamp an intentional radiator: in the US that means a certified FCC ID, not a self-declared SDoC.

  • Japan requires the MIC giteki mark per model, and PSE obligations are tied to a Japanese responsible entity.

What certifications does a Bluetooth speaker lamp need in the US, EU and Japan?

Three approval tracks apply at once: radio (FCC ID, CE-RED, MIC giteki), electrical and photobiological safety for the light, and Bluetooth SIG qualification for the mark.

The most common first-time importer mistake is budgeting for a lamp and then discovering that a 2.4 GHz module drags in a second, considerably more expensive approval stack. A luminaire brief assumes a passive product: driver, LEDs, housing, plug. The moment a radio goes inside, the same object is also radio equipment, and radio rules are written around the transmitter — conducted power, antenna gain, spurious emissions, RF exposure — none of which a lighting lab will test on its own initiative.

The US route: a certified FCC ID, not a self-declaration

Bluetooth is an intentional radiator, so the finished product needs certification through a Telecommunication Certification Body, with a grantee code held in your company's name. The LED driver and any digital control board fall under Part 15 Subpart B as unintentional radiators and are handled with a supplier's declaration of conformity. Retail adds a layer that is not law: Amazon and most big-box buyers want a UL or ETL listing from an NRTL for anything that plugs into 120 V, and that listing is issued against the product, not the factory.

Europe: one CE mark, several directives behind it

RED 2014/53/EU is the umbrella for the radio, and because it already carries essential requirements for safety and electromagnetic compatibility, mains-powered radio equipment generally does not need a duplicate Low Voltage Directive certificate. That single distinction saves both money and a redundant test report. What RED does not cover, you still need: RoHS, WEEE registration in each member state you ship to, REACH, the ecodesign and energy labelling rules that apply to the light source, and the General Product Safety Regulation, which since 13 December 2024 requires a named EU responsible person on the product or its packaging.

Japan: per-model giteki, plus a local entity for PSE

The Radio Law requires the technical conformity mark — giteki — for each radio model, granted through a registered certification body, and there is no self-declaration shortcut for most Bluetooth consumer products. AC adapters sit under DENAN/PSE, and that notification is tied to a Japanese supplier of record, so either your distributor holds it or you appoint a service to act for you. VCCI membership is voluntary, but Japanese retail channels expect the mark on the box.

Why does adding Bluetooth change the compliance scope of a lamp so much?

Because radio rules, lighting rules and battery rules are separate regimes, and a Bluetooth lamp is one of the few products that has to satisfy all of them at the same time.

RF exposure is the item first-time buyers forget

A bedside speaker lamp lives within arm's reach of a sleeping adult, which pushes the lab to make an explicit exposure judgement rather than assume a generic 20 cm separation. If the antenna sits at the top of the shade, the realistic distance to a hand is small enough that some labs will require a SAR assessment instead of a simple MPE calculation. Antenna position, housing material and the metal content of the trim all feed into that decision, and it is far cheaper to settle it at the mechanical design stage than after tooling.

Cybersecurity became a CE requirement in August 2025

The RED delegated regulation on cybersecurity, privacy and fraud applies from 1 August 2025 and points to the EN 18031 series as the presumption of conformity. App-controlled Bluetooth lamps sit in a grey zone: a pure Bluetooth link is not internet-connected, but the companion app usually is, and labs are interpreting that differently. In practice you want per-device passwords, no universal default credential, a documented update policy and an honest data-flow description — the same evidence satisfies ETSI EN 303 645 and US state laws such as California SB-327 in most cases.

The battery is a third product inside the product

A rechargeable speaker lamp carries a lithium cell, which brings UN 38.3 test documentation for transport, IEC 62133-2 for cell and pack safety, and the EU Batteries Regulation 2023/1542 with its labelling, collection and — from 18 February 2027 — removability requirements for portable batteries. Charging is its own question: Directive (EU) 2022/2380 extended common-charger rules to portable speakers from 28 December 2024, so if your unit is classified that way, plan USB-C PD rather than a proprietary barrel jack. Confirm the classification with your lab before the ID is fixed.

Light-specific tests a speaker OEM will not think to ask for

Photobiological safety under IEC 62471 is where blue-light hazard gets assessed, and most mood-lighting designs aim for the exempt group. Flicker is a real rejection risk: EU Regulation 2019/2020 sets limits for light sources at SVM ≤ 0.4 and Pst LM ≤ 1, and cheap PWM dimming will fail both. Electrically, a combined unit usually needs IEC 62368-1 for the audio and electronic assembly alongside IEC 60598-1 for the luminaire and IEC 61347 for the driver. All of this has to coexist with thermal reality — an LED and a battery sharing a sealed shade is a design problem long before it is a testing problem.

Do I need Bluetooth SIG qualification to sell a speaker lamp?

Yes. Bluetooth is a licensed trademark and a licensed technology stack, and every shipping model needs a Declaration ID recorded in the SIG database.

The mechanics are unglamorous but easy to plan. You join the Bluetooth SIG at Adopter level, you qualify the design against the Core specification and the profiles you actually implement — A2DP and AVRCP for a music lamp, HFP only if you are adding speakerphone — and you file a Declaration ID that lists the product. Industry-typical fees are a membership charge plus a per-declaration charge, and the exact figures move, so confirm current pricing with the SIG rather than with a factory quotation.

Module QDID versus your own declaration

Using a pre-qualified Bluetooth module lets you reference the module vendor's QDID for the radio and link layer, which shortens both the SIG process and the lab work. It does not exempt your product from radio testing: the antenna matching, ground plane and enclosure you designed around that module change the emissions profile, so FCC and RED testing still happen on the finished unit. Buyers who assume a module certificate covers the lamp are the ones who discover the gap two weeks before a launch.

What forces a re-declaration or re-test

Changing the radio module, the antenna, the profile set or the RF-relevant PCB layout invalidates what you already paid for. Changing a colourway, a fabric shade or a non-RF plastic trim normally does not, which is why colour and finish variants should be declared in the original filing as a model family. If you plan a mid-life refresh, freeze the radio design first and treat everything else as cosmetic.

Now the arithmetic nobody enjoys. Certification is priced per model, not per unit, so a 100 pcs pilot carries exactly the same test fee as a 10,000 pcs run. At pilot volume the certification cost per lamp can easily exceed the manufacturing cost per lamp. Most sensible buyers respond by starting with FCC and CE only, keeping the first order to a single model family and one or two finishes, and adding Japan, Korea and Australia once reorders are predictable. Who actually owns the certificate — me or the factory?

You should own it. Product approvals must be issued in your brand's name and your grantee code, or you cannot enforce, transfer or defend them.

The confusion usually comes from mixing two different kinds of paper. Site and process credentials belong to the factory: LIGHT BDB holds ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL registered factory status and Sony Green Partner. Product approvals belong to whoever is named on them, and if that is a trading company you do not control, you are renting your market access. Ask for the grantee code, the certificate holder field and the EU responsible person name before you sign, not after.

What are the honest trade-offs of building Bluetooth into a lamp?

Four real ones: certification cost lands per model, antennas fight metal shades, the app outlives the tooling, and battery heat sits beside the driver.

  • Optics and acoustics pull against each other. A shade that diffuses light beautifully is usually a poor speaker chamber. Expect to trade some bass extension for light output, or some diffusion for volume — a good ODM will show you both curves before tooling rather than after.
  • Every new enclosure is a new filing. Three shapes with three antennas is three certification budgets, not one. If cost matters more than range width, launch one shape properly and expand later.
  • Metal looks premium and behaves badly. Aluminium trim near the antenna detunes it, and the fix is usually mechanical. Settle the antenna placement before the industrial design is signed off, not after.
  • The app is a long-term liability. Firmware, cloud endpoints and phone OS updates will outlive your tooling by years. Budget for maintenance, or choose a design where the Bluetooth link is optional and the lamp still works as a lamp.
  • MOQ floors are real. At 100 pcs we are not the cheapest option on the market, and we will say so. Very small runs are better served by white-label stock than by custom tooling.
  • We stay out of regulated healthcare territory. LIGHT BDB is a wellness-device ODM and we decline briefs that need regulated healthcare claims — that is a different supply chain with a different approval system, and pretending otherwise would waste your money.

Below are the questions our US, EU and Japanese buyers send most often before a first speaker lamp order.

Does a Bluetooth speaker lamp need FCC certification?

Yes. Bluetooth is an intentional radiator, so the finished product needs an FCC ID issued through a Telecommunication Certification Body, with the grantee code in your company's name. The LED driver separately needs a Part 15 Subpart B supplier's declaration of conformity.

Can I use one FCC ID for several colourways of the same speaker lamp?

Usually yes, if the radio module, antenna, PCB layout and electrical design are identical and the variants are declared as a model family in the original filing. A different housing material near the antenna, or a different driver, is a new filing.

Is CE marking alone enough to sell a Bluetooth lamp in Europe?

CE marking under RED covers the radio and folds in safety and EMC objectives, but it is not the whole package. You still need RoHS compliance, WEEE registration per member state, REACH, the ecodesign and energy labelling rules for the light source, and a named EU responsible person under GPSR.

Do I have to join the Bluetooth SIG to sell a Bluetooth speaker?

Yes. Bluetooth is a licensed trademark and technology, so you need Adopter-level membership and a Declaration ID for each shipping model before you use the word mark or logo. Using a pre-qualified module simplifies the process but does not remove the declaration requirement.

How much does it cost to certify a Bluetooth speaker lamp?

Industry-typical budgets quoted by factories in this category run in the mid-four figures in USD per model for FCC and EU-RED together, and toward five figures when Japan, Korea and Australia are added. Those figures are per model family, and colour variants declared in the same filing do not repeat the cost.

How long does certification take for a Bluetooth lamp?

Testing itself is industry-typically 3–6 weeks per market after design freeze, and markets can run in parallel if the lab holds the right scope. Add the Bluetooth SIG declaration and any retail listing audit on top, and budget a full quarter from approved sample to shelf-ready paperwork.

Does a rechargeable speaker lamp need UN 38.3 testing?

Yes. Lithium cells and packs need UN 38.3 test documentation for transport, and IEC 62133-2 covers cell and pack safety. In Europe the Batteries Regulation 2023/1542 adds labelling and collection duties, with removability requirements for portable batteries applying from 18 February 2027.

Can a factory's CE certificate cover my brand's product?

Not properly. Product approvals must be issued in the name of the party that places the goods on the market, which is you. Factory-held credentials such as ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI or UL registered factory status support your filing but do not substitute for it.

Do I need a Japanese entity to sell a Bluetooth lamp in Japan?

The MIC giteki mark is granted per radio model, but PSE obligations for the AC adapter are tied to a Japanese notifying supplier of record. Either your distributor holds that role or you appoint a service; the giteki mark alone does not complete the paperwork.

Which safety standard applies to a lamp with a built-in speaker?

A combined unit is normally assessed against IEC 62368-1 for the electronic and audio assembly, IEC 60598-1 for the luminaire, IEC 61347 for the driver and IEC 62471 for photobiological safety. A competent lab will scope all four; a lighting-only lab often will not.
