What is Prop 65 and which chemicals in wellness devices trigger the warning requirement?

Prop 65 is a California right-to-know law that requires a warning when a product exposes users to any of roughly 900 listed chemicals above a safe-harbor level. In wellness devices, the usual triggers are lead, cadmium, phthalates, and flame retardants.

Enacted in 1986 as the Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act, Prop 65 applies to any person or business "in the course of doing business" that sells or distributes products in California. That includes brands outside the US: if you ship a heated massager, red light therapy panel, or light-and-mood lamp to a California address, the law applies to you.

OEHHA, the California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment, maintains the chemical list and updates it at least once a year. About 300 of the listed chemicals have safe-harbor levels. A product that exposes a user below that level needs no warning; above it, the warning is required regardless of how the product is used.

What Prop 65 is not

Prop 65 is not a product safety standard, a ban, or a certification. A product with a warning label can be completely safe when used as intended. The law only requires that you inform the consumer that a listed chemical is present above a very conservative threshold.

The chemicals that matter most for your category

  • Lead — solder joints on printed circuit boards, brass fittings, and some pigments. Heated massagers with control boards, chargers, and motors are typical candidates.
  • Cadmium — chip packaging, plating on small metal parts, and some plastic pigments.
  • Phthalates — PVC power cords, soft-touch grips, and flexible tubing. DEHP and DBP are the most commonly listed forms.
  • Flame retardants — enclosure plastics, foam padding, and adhesives. TDCPP and TCEP appear in many consumer electronics.
  • Bisphenol A (BPA) — epoxy resins and protective coatings on some components.

For red light therapy devices, check the LED driver board, the casing, and the cable first. For heated massagers, add the heating element wiring and the control interface. If your ODM can document those components, you have covered most of the risk.

What does a compliant Prop 65 warning label actually look like?

A compliant warning must include the word WARNING, the name of the chemical, and a statement that the product exposes the user to it. OEHHA publishes safe-harbor wording; using it gives you a presumption of compliance.

The classic label pairs a yellow warning symbol with text like this: "WARNING: This product can expose you to lead, which is known to the State of California to cause cancer. For more information go to www.P65Warnings.ca.gov." For reproductive harm, the wording changes to "birth defects or other reproductive harm." When both apply, the label can say "Cancer and Reproductive Harm."

The short-form warning

For small products, OEHHA allows a short-form warning: "WARNING: Cancer and Reproductive Harm – www.P65Warnings.ca.gov." It is permitted when the label area is 5 square inches or less, and it does not name the specific chemical. The full-form warning is the safer choice for most wellness devices because it gives the consumer more information and reduces legal ambiguity.

Placement

The warning can go on the product, the packaging, or both, as long as it is "clear and reasonable." For online sales, a warning on the product page or a link to the full warning also satisfies the standard. Many brands apply the label at the factory so every unit ships ready for California.

Who writes the wording

You do not need a lawyer to draft the label if you use OEHHA's safe-harbor text verbatim. Custom wording is riskier because a private plaintiff can argue it is not "clear and reasonable." Most first-time importers use the safe-harbor wording and spend their budget on material data instead.

Who is legally responsible — the brand owner or the ODM factory?

The brand owner is legally responsible for Prop 65 compliance; a factory can supply data and apply labels but cannot take the liability. Enforcement targets the seller, and in practice that means your brand.

Prop 65 is enforced by the California Attorney General, district attorneys, and private plaintiffs. Most cases are filed by private plaintiffs, sometimes called bounty hunters, who must serve a 60-day notice of intent to sue before going to court. That notice gives you a window to correct the label and settle.

Your ODM's role is to give you the facts you need:

  • Component-level material declarations for the bill of materials.
  • Restricted-substance test reports from recognized labs.
  • Label application during assembly — most factories in this category can do this.

How do I get the material data I need from my ODM partner?

Ask your ODM for component-level material declarations and restricted-substance test reports before you commit to a purchase order. Written declarations are the backbone of your compliance file.

Request these items in writing:

  • A bill-of-materials declaration listing every component and its material composition.
  • RoHS and REACH test reports as supporting evidence. They are not Prop 65 proof, but they show which substances are present.
  • XRF screening results for lead and cadmium on the housing, cable, and circuit board.
  • Supplier declarations for cables, plastics, adhesives, and packaging inserts.

RoHS and REACH data is useful but not conclusive for Prop 65, because the chemical lists and thresholds differ. If your ODM cannot provide declarations, send samples to a third-party lab such as Intertek, SGS, or Bureau Veritas. Industry-typical costs run from a few hundred dollars for XRF screening to a few thousand for full wet-chemistry analysis per SKU.

What are the honest trade-offs and risks in Prop 65 compliance?

The honest trade-off is that Prop 65 compliance costs money and a warning label can make a safe product look scarier than it is. Both over-warning and under-warning carry real costs.

Over-warning is common: many brands label conservatively because testing every component is expensive. The downside is label fatigue — consumers see warnings everywhere and stop reading them, and a warning on a harmless product can hurt sales. Under-warning is riskier: penalties run up to $2,500 per violation per day, and the bounty-hunter structure means someone has a financial incentive to find your product.

Three practical realities to accept:

  • Prop 65 is a disclosure law, not a safety verdict. A warning does not mean your product is unsafe, but you will have to explain that to retailers and customers.
  • The chemical list changes every year. A product that needs no warning today may need one next year, so review your labels annually.
  • Your ODM can give you data and apply labels, but it cannot make the legal decision for you. Have a California-qualified attorney review your wording at least once.

What are the most common Prop 65 questions from first-time importers?

Here are the questions buyers ask most often about Prop 65, answered directly.

Does Prop 65 apply to my product if I only sell online?

Yes. Prop 65 applies to any business selling products into California, including e-commerce. If you ship to California customers, the product should carry a warning or the product page should display one. A label inside the package plus an online warning is the most common approach for direct-to-consumer brands.

What happens if I sell in California without a Prop 65 warning?

You face penalties of up to $2,500 per violation per day. Most enforcement comes from private plaintiffs, who must send a 60-day notice before filing a lawsuit. That notice gives you a window to correct the labeling and settle before the case escalates.

Is there a list of Prop 65 chemicals I can check?

Yes. OEHHA publishes the list, which includes roughly 900 chemicals and is updated at least once a year. The most relevant entries for electronic wellness products are lead, cadmium, phthalates, and certain flame retardants.

Can my factory in China handle Prop 65 labeling for me?

A factory can apply the label and provide material declarations, but the legal responsibility stays with the brand owner. LIGHT BDB's Shenzhen plant handles finished-product assembly, so label application can be part of the production plan. Have your own counsel review the final wording before you commit.

What is the difference between Prop 65 and RoHS or REACH?

RoHS and REACH are chemical restriction regimes for the European market, while Prop 65 is a California right-to-know law. Test data from RoHS or REACH programs is useful supporting evidence, but it does not automatically prove Prop 65 compliance because the chemical lists and exposure thresholds differ.

How much does Prop 65 compliance testing cost?

Costs vary by product and lab. An XRF screening for lead and cadmium typically runs a few hundred dollars per sample, while full wet-chemistry analysis costs more; most brands budget roughly $500 to $2,500 per SKU for initial screening. Ask your ODM for existing declarations first, because that can reduce the testing you need.

Do I need a Prop 65 warning on the product itself or is the box enough?

The warning can be on the product, its packaging, or both, as long as it is clear and reasonable. Small products may use a short-form label or a hang tag. Many brands put the label on the unit at the factory and also show the warning on the product page.

Does a Prop 65 warning mean my product is unsafe?

No. Prop 65 is a right-to-know law, not a safety standard. A product with a warning can be completely safe when used as intended; the warning exists because the product contains a listed chemical above a very conservative threshold.

What is the short-form Prop 65 warning?

The short-form warning reads: WARNING: Cancer and Reproductive Harm - www.P65Warnings.ca.gov. It is allowed on products with a label area of 5 square inches or less and does not name the specific chemical. The full-form warning names the chemical and is the safer choice for most wellness devices.

Who enforces Prop 65?

The California Attorney General and district attorneys can enforce the law, but most cases are filed by private plaintiffs, sometimes called bounty hunters. They must serve a 60-day notice before suing, which gives you time to come into compliance and settle.

Can I use one warning label for all my products?

Only if every SKU contains the same listed chemicals at similar exposure levels. A single generic warning is common, but if one product contains lead and another contains phthalates, each label should name the relevant chemical. Material declarations from your ODM help you decide per SKU.
