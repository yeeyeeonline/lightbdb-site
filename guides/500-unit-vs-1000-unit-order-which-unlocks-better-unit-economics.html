How much lower is the unit cost at 1,000 units versus 500 units?

Moving from 500 to 1,000 units typically reduces per-unit cost by 10–15% — an industry-typical spread, since LIGHT BDB quotes exact pricing per SKU. Exact percentages depend on the product, component choices, and packaging. What matters is where that saving comes from.

What drives the cost difference?

  • Component pricing: a 1,000-unit order lets suppliers quote better per-part rates, especially for PCBs, batteries, and housings.
  • Amortized tooling and moulds: fixed costs spread across twice as many units.
  • Fewer production changeovers: one continuous run at 1,000 units avoids the setup costs of splitting into multiple batches.
  • Logistics per unit: freight and handling costs are largely fixed, so each unit carries a smaller share.

Where does the advantage shrink?

The 10–15% saving only helps if you sell through the extra 500 units within a reasonable window. Holding 1,000 units for six months erases the margin gain through warehousing, financing, and price-markdown risk. If your channel absorbs 500 units in 60–90 days, the Pro tier is usually the right call. If not, the Slim tier protects you better.

At 1,000 units you get full customization — shell colors, logo placement, packaging, and firmware where applicable — while 500-unit orders typically stay within existing ODM options. LIGHT BDB's design centers in Hangzhou and Hamburg support the product design work, and the Panyu plant supplies components in-house.

For a private-label brand, full customization matters beyond cosmetics:

  • Proprietary shell colors and textures make your product harder to copy.
  • Custom firmware can lock in feature sets and power profiles for red light therapy devices.
  • Private-label packaging controls unboxing experience, which drives repeat purchases and reviews.
  • Component selection — like wireless charging coils — can differentiate the product in a crowded market.

At 500 units, you can still brand the product with logos and some color choices, but you are typically working within existing tooling. That keeps you faster to market and lower risk, at the cost of brand distinctiveness.

Should a first-time importer order 1,000 units or start at 500?

Start at 500 units unless you can confidently forecast sell-through within 60–90 days; then move to 1,000 to capture better unit economics. Ask yourself these five questions before deciding:

  1. Do you have confirmed distribution — retail POs, Amazon history, or a pre-order campaign?
  2. Can your warehouse or 3PL hold 1,000 units without straining cash flow?
  3. Is the product a repeat-purchase item, or a one-time gift purchase?
  4. Will you need to iterate on design after customer feedback?
  5. Are you comfortable holding 500 extra units if sell-through is slow?

If you answer yes to questions 1–3 and no to question 4, the Pro tier is worth the cash. If you are uncertain on any point, the Slim tier at 500 units is the safer path — and it is the most popular choice at LIGHT BDB for exactly that reason.

What are the honest trade-offs of ordering 1,000 units before proving demand?

Ordering 1,000 units before proving demand puts cash, warehouse space, and design flexibility at risk — a lower unit price is worthless if inventory sits unsold. Here are the trade-offs you should weigh honestly:

  • Cash flow: T/T terms mean roughly 30% at deposit and 70% before shipment. 1,000 units ties up more capital before you see revenue.
  • Storage: more units mean higher warehousing and insurance costs, especially for larger wellness devices like heated massagers.
  • Design lock-in: full custom tooling at 1,000 units makes it expensive to change the shell or firmware after v1 feedback.
  • Margin illusion: the 10–15% unit saving disappears if markdowns or holding costs eat into it.

These are real risks, and a good ODM partner should name them. LIGHT BDB addresses some of the downside with a 500-day limited warranty, refundable samples, and tier flexibility that lets you start at Slim and graduate to Pro after a successful run. But no tier structure removes the fundamental truth: order size only saves money if you sell through.
