When did RoHS take effect, and exactly which substances does it restrict?

RoHS took effect on July 1, 2006, restricting six hazardous substances in electrical and electronic equipment (EEE) sold in the EU.

The original RoHS Directive (2002/95/EC) limited six substances in homogeneous materials: lead, mercury, cadmium, hexavalent chromium, polybrominated biphenyls (PBB), and polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDE). Maximum concentrations are 0.1% by weight for all except cadmium, which is capped at 0.01%.

In July 2011, the EU replaced it with RoHS 2 (Directive 2011/65/EU), which became mandatory on January 2, 2013. RoHS 2 widened the product scope, added CE-marking obligations, and phased in some product categories on later timelines.

The 2019 phthalate expansion

On July 22, 2019, Directive (EU) 2015/863 added four phthalates to the restriction list: DEHP, BBP, DBP, and DIBP. Every EEE sold in the EU from that date must stay under the 0.1% limit for these substances as well. If you import a heated massager or red-light device today, all ten substances apply.

  • Lead, mercury, cadmium, hexavalent chromium — restricted since July 2006.
  • PBB and PBDE flame retardants — restricted since July 2006.
  • DEHP, BBP, DBP, DIBP phthalates — restricted since July 22, 2019.
  • RoHS 2 requires a Declaration of Conformity and CE marking for most EEE.

What is Sony Green Partner certification, and how does it go beyond RoHS?

Sony Green Partner is Sony's private supply-chain certification, active since July 2002, and it audits factories against a stricter substance list than RoHS.

Sony launched the Green Partner program to control hazardous chemicals across its entire supplier base. Certified factories receive a specific Green Partner number, and that status is revocable — Sony re-audits suppliers on a regular cycle and can suspend or cancel certification for non-conformance.

The substance list difference

Where RoHS restricts 10 substances, Sony's standard (SS-00259, now part of the Sony Group Green Procurement Guidelines) covers roughly 27 substance groups. That broader scope includes materials RoHS does not touch, such as certain chlorinated flame retardants, polyvinyl chloride (PVC), beryllium and its compounds, arsenic compounds, and antimony compounds. Sony also sets thresholds for some substances below the RoHS limits.

The practical takeaway: Sony Green Partner is a stricter, audit-based version of material compliance. A factory that holds it has proven it can control a wider chemical list under recurring third-party scrutiny — which is why many global brands treat it as a trust signal even outside Sony's supply chain.

Why should a private-label wellness brand care about RoHS and Sony Green Partner?

RoHS compliance is legally required to sell electronics in the EU, and Sony Green Partner shows a factory passes a stricter audit many global brands trust.

If your heated massager, red-light panel, or mood lamp contains a printed circuit board, wiring, plastics, or a charger, RoHS applies the moment the first unit lands in the EU. Non-compliance can mean customs holds, product seizure, recall costs, and fines — all without a single customer complaint.

How can a first-time importer verify that a factory is genuinely compliant?

Ask for the current certificate, verify the audit date and issuing body, then request a substance test report for your specific SKU.

Compliance paperwork is cheap to fake, so verify deliberately. Here is the practical checklist most experienced importers use:

  • Request the certificate PDF with the factory's legal name exactly matching your contract party.
  • Check the issue date — most environmental certifications are renewed annually; anything older than three years is a red flag.
  • Ask for the substance test report (industry practice uses IEC 62321 test methods) for the materials in your product: housing plastic, cable, PCB, and solder.
  • Confirm your SKU's bill of materials is covered — a certificate for one product line does not cover another.
  • For Sony Green Partner, ask for the supplier's Green Partner number and confirm it with Sony's published supplier list if possible.

What are the honest trade-offs and limits of RoHS and Sony Green Partner?

RoHS and Sony Green Partner restrict chemicals in materials — they do not certify finished-product safety, performance, or health effects.

You should treat both standards as a compliance floor, not a complete quality package. They do not cover electrical safety, electromagnetic compatibility, packaging waste rules, or functional durability. A product can be RoHS-compliant and still fail a drop test or an electrical safety inspection in your target market.

  • Certificates expire and audits repeat — always confirm the current status, not the oldest PDF in a sales folder.
  • Sony Green Partner is a procurement gate for Sony's supply chain; it is not a general market certification and does not replace country-specific electrical safety marks.
  • Compliance testing adds cost and schedule. Budget for your own lab test on the first production batch; a supplier's report is a starting point, not a substitute.
  • Neither standard says anything about a product's wellness or performance claims. LIGHT BDB's certifications cover quality and environmental management — no product in the catalog carries a health-treatment claim.

None of this makes RoHS or Sony Green Partner less useful. It just means a smart buyer uses them as screening tools, then adds independent testing and clear specifications on the first order.
