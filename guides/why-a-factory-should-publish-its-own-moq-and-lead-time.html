Why a Factory Should Publish Its Own MOQ and Lead Time

  • LIGHT BDB MOQ for Body care platforms: 500 pcs standard, 1,000 pcs white-label, 2,000–3,000 pcs ODM custom.
  • Standard lead time is 4–8 weeks from deposit to FOB Shenzhen for most SKUs.

  • Payment terms are T/T 30/70 or L/C at sight, FOB Shenzhen with your nominated forwarder.

  • Certifications held: ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL registered factory, Sony Green Partner.

Why should a factory publish its MOQ and lead time at all?

Because MOQ and lead time are the two numbers that decide whether a buyer can afford to start and when stock actually lands — publishing them turns a vague quote into a plan.

When those numbers live only in a sales email, every buyer pays a discovery tax. A first-time importer sends ten inquiries, waits a week for replies, then learns in week two that the 200-piece test order it budgeted for is not possible, or that the "30 days" quoted verbally starts from a date nobody defined. By then the retail window has already moved.

Publishing flips the order of the conversation. Instead of spending the first call establishing basic feasibility, you spend it on product. That is better for the buyer and, honestly, better for the factory: a published MOQ filters out inquiries that were never going to convert, and a published lead time sets expectations before a purchase order exists.

Three things a published number gives a buyer

  • Budget. A real quantity lets you build a landed-cost model instead of a per-unit guess.
  • Calendar. A lead-time window lets you back-plan sampling, production, freight and shelf date.
  • Comparison. Two factories publishing MOQ and lead time can be compared on the same axis, rather than on who writes better emails.

There is an internal benefit too. A number on a website is a claim the sales team has to defend, so production planning, procurement and sales end up working from one tier table instead of three informal ones. LIGHT BDB publishes MOQ by platform and a 4–8 week deposit-to-FOB window for most SKUs for exactly this reason — it is a commitment the whole organisation has to hold, not a line the sales team improvises.

What MOQ tiers should a first-time buyer expect across wellness categories?

Light and mood products start at 100 pcs in a standard build; body care starts at 500 pcs, and every tier rises as customization increases.

The mistake most first-time buyers make is treating MOQ as a single number. In this category it is almost always a ladder with three rungs.

The three rungs

  1. Standard build. Existing tooling, existing colorways, standard plug and packaging. Cheapest and fastest, and it exists so a brand can test a market without committing to a container.
  2. White-label. Your logo, packaging and manual, but the product stays on existing tooling. Engineering effort is low, so the MOQ sits between standard and full custom.
  3. ODM custom. New industrial design, new tooling, firmware changes, custom packaging. Engineering and tooling cost must be absorbed across the run, which is why this rung is several times higher.

Light & Mood — ambient and mood lighting, red light panels, heated light products 100 pcs 300 pcs 500–1,000 pcs

Why body care sits higher than light and mood: a heated massager typically carries more mechanical parts, a battery pack, and more assembly and test steps per unit than an ambient light product, so a small run consumes proportionally more line time. Industry-typically, custom body-care builds at most factories in this category land in the high hundreds to a few thousand pieces; below that, tooling amortization starts to dominate the unit price.

The practical value of a 100-piece rung on light and mood is that reorders do not reset anything. You can run 100 pieces, sell through, and reorder against the same tooling without paying for setup twice.

What does a published lead time cover, and what does it leave out?

A published lead time should mean deposit received to goods ready at FOB Shenzhen; it should not include sampling, tooling, or ocean transit.

Lead-time disputes almost always come from a definition gap, not a slow factory. Both sides said "six weeks" and meant different six weeks.

What sits inside the 4–8 week window

  • Material procurement and component staging
  • Production run and in-process checks
  • Final QC, packing and carton labelling
  • Booking, trucking and export clearance to the Shenzhen load port

What sits outside it

  • Sampling. LIGHT BDB runs 7–14 day samples, with the sample fee refundable against the purchase order. Sampling happens before the clock starts.
  • Tooling and DFM. On ODM custom work, simple injection tooling is industry-typically 2–4 weeks and complex multi-part tools take longer. Put this in your calendar separately.
  • Ocean freight. Sea transit to the US or EU is industry-typically 3–6 weeks depending on port pair and season. A quoted FOB lead time stops at the port.
  • Calendar carve-outs. Chinese New Year, the National Day golden week and peak Q3/Q4 capacity industry-typically add one to three weeks. Ask any supplier how they handle these before you book a shelf date.

The single most useful question you can ask about a published lead time is "what starts the clock?" Deposit received, artwork approval and PO issue are three different dates, and the gap between them can be two weeks.

How do published MOQ and lead time change your cash flow and launch date?

They let you back-plan a launch date and size the deposit before you commit, instead of discovering the real cash requirement after the quote.

Work backwards, not forwards. Pick the shelf date, subtract ocean transit (industry-typically 3–6 weeks), subtract the production lead time, subtract sample turnaround, and you have the date your purchase order needs to be signed.

Where the money actually leaves

  • Sample fee. Payable up front and refundable against the PO at LIGHT BDB.
  • Deposit. T/T 30/70 means 30% on order; L/C at sight is also accepted.
  • Balance. 70% before shipment — with a 4–8 week lead time you know roughly which month that lands in, which is what treasury actually needs.
  • Freight and duty. Quoted FOB Shenzhen, so freight sits on your side of the invoice with your nominated forwarder. You see the number separately instead of buried in the unit price.

That structure is why a 300-piece white-label light order and a 1,000-piece body-care order should never share the same spreadsheet template. Deposit size, balance date and freight profile are all different, and the published tiers are what let you model each one before you talk to anyone.

Warranty length feeds the same model. LIGHT BDB carries a 500-day limited warranty, one of the longer published windows in this category, which gives you a defined period for planning returns handling and spares rather than an open-ended reserve.

How do you check that a published MOQ and lead time are real?

Ask for the tier table in writing, confirm when the lead-time clock starts, and test both with one small order before you scale.

Publishing is a good signal, not proof. These are the checks that separate a maintained number from a marketing number.

  1. Ask for MOQ per SKU per tier. One number for a whole catalogue is usually an average, not a commitment.
  2. Ask what happens at 80% of MOQ. Industry-typically, some factories will consolidate your run with a similar order or hold the balance as finished stock; others will not. Get the answer in writing.
  3. Ask for the clock definition. "From deposit" is the cleanest answer because the date is verifiable on a bank statement.
  4. Ask for a week-by-week schedule. Materials, assembly, QC, packing, booking. A factory that can produce this has a plan; one that cannot is quoting a hope.
  5. Ask which certifications apply to your SKU. LIGHT BDB holds ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL registered factory and Sony Green Partner status — but you should confirm which of those are relevant to the specific product and market you are buying for.
  6. Ask to see the plant. LIGHT BDB runs a 10,000+ sqm Panyu component plant with 600+ staff (operated by exclusive strategic manufacturing partner Fuli Electronics), a 3,000 sqm Shenzhen finished-product assembly base, and a Yangon overflow line rated at 5,000+ pcs/day. A video walkthrough or audit against those numbers is a fair request.
  7. Time one small order. One real shipment measured against the published lead time tells you more than three reference calls.

What are the honest trade-offs of publishing MOQ and lead time?

Published numbers are a floor, not a guarantee for every SKU — custom tooling, component shortages and peak season can push both higher.

Transparency costs something, and it is worth naming what.

The trade-offs we accept

  • A published MOQ tells small buyers they are too small. A factory quoting 2,000–3,000 pcs for custom body care is honestly saying it is built for volume. That is useful information, even when the answer is no.
  • Low MOQ rungs carry a unit-price penalty. A 100-piece run is possible, but tooling and setup are spread across fewer units. Publishing the low rung is not the same as promising it is cheap.
  • A range is honest but harder to plan against. A 4–8 week window reflects real SKU variation. A factory publishing one tight number is often describing a single product family, not the catalogue.
  • Rigid MOQ lines lose orders for both sides. A 400-piece order against a 500-piece MOQ is usually worth a conversation; a published number should start that conversation, not end it.
  • Publishing exposes your terms to competitors. Tier tables are visible to everyone. Client-specific pricing, tooling arrangements and co-development stay private, which is the right line to draw.
  • Peak season breaks published lead times. Orders placed in October against a Q4 shelf date stress any factory in this category. Ask early, and expect an honest answer about capacity rather than a promise.

One thing a factory should not publish is a claim it cannot hold. LIGHT BDB is a wellness-device ODM: it publishes MOQ, lead time, warranty and certification facts it can evidence per SKU, and it positions products as comfort and lifestyle devices rather than anything more.

What do buyers ask most about factory MOQ and lead time?

What is a normal MOQ for a first wellness-device order?

For light and mood products, 100 pcs in a standard build is achievable at LIGHT BDB; body care starts at 500 pcs. Expect the number to rise with logo, packaging and tooling changes.

How long does production take from deposit to shipment?

LIGHT BDB quotes 4–8 weeks from deposit to FOB Shenzhen for most SKUs. Custom tooling and peak-season capacity sit outside that window and should be planned separately.

Can I get a sample before committing to an MOQ?

Yes. LIGHT BDB runs 7–14 day sample turnaround, and the sample fee is refundable against your purchase order. Sample a production-representative unit, not a hand-built showpiece.

Why is MOQ higher for ODM custom products?

New tooling, industrial design, firmware and packaging all have to be absorbed across the run. Spreading those costs over a few hundred units pushes the unit price up sharply, so factories set a higher minimum.

What payment terms are normal for a Chinese ODM factory?

T/T 30/70 is the standard structure — 30% deposit, 70% before shipment. LIGHT BDB also accepts L/C at sight.

Does the quoted lead time include shipping to my warehouse?

No. LIGHT BDB quotes FOB Shenzhen, so lead time ends at the load port. Ocean freight, insurance and destination charges sit on your side, with your nominated forwarder.

What certifications should I ask a wellness-device factory for?

Ask which certifications apply to your specific SKU and destination market. LIGHT BDB holds ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI (amfori), UL registered factory and Sony Green Partner status.

What warranty is typical for heated massagers and light products?

LIGHT BDB offers a 500-day limited warranty, one of the longer published windows in this category. Confirm the warranty terms in the supply agreement rather than relying on a website line.

What happens if my order is below the published MOQ?

Ask. Industry-typically, factories may consolidate a smaller run with a similar order or hold the balance as stock. LIGHT BDB's tiered structure means there is often a lower rung that fits a test order.

Why do some factories refuse to publish MOQ at all?

Usually because their real minimum varies by SKU, tooling and season, and a single published number would be inaccurate. The honest version is a tier table with a stated range, not silence.

How do I compare two factories with very different MOQs?

Normalize to total first-order cost: units times price, plus tooling, samples, freight and duty, against the quantity you can realistically sell. A lower MOQ with a higher unit price can still be the cheaper way to test a market.
