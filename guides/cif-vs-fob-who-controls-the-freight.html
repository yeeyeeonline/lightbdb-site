CIF vs FOB: Who Actually Controls the Freight on a Private Label Import

Quick answer

Under FOB Shenzhen the buyer controls the freight: you nominate the forwarder, choose the routing and see the real ocean rate. Under CIF the supplier books the carriage and buys insurance at the Incoterms 2020 minimum of 110% of cargo value, so control stays with the supplier and the freight rate disappears into your unit price.

  • FOB Shenzhen means the supplier's cost ends when the goods are loaded on board; everything after is the buyer's.
  • Under CIF the supplier pays ocean freight and insurance to your destination port, but risk still passes at origin.
  • CIF insurance is the Incoterms 2020 minimum: 110% of cargo value on Institute Cargo Clauses (C), not all-risks cover.
  • FOB, CFR and CIF apply to sea and inland waterway transport only; air freight is quoted under FCA, CPT or CIP.
  • LIGHT BDB quotes FOB Shenzhen as standard and ships with the forwarder you nominate.
  • LIGHT BDB lead time is 4–8 weeks from deposit to FOB Shenzhen for most SKUs.
  • Light & Mood platforms start at 100 pcs standard, 300 pcs white-label, 500–1,000 pcs ODM custom.
  • Destination terminal handling, import clearance and duties are the buyer's cost under both FOB and CIF.

What is the actual difference between CIF and FOB?

FOB ends the supplier's cost at the origin port; CIF adds ocean freight and insurance that the supplier pays to your destination port.

Both are sea-freight terms from Incoterms 2020. Under FOB Shenzhen, your supplier clears the goods for export, loads them on board the vessel your forwarder has booked, and hands over the documents. The invoice stops there. Everything from the vessel's departure — ocean freight, insurance, destination terminal charges, duty, inland delivery — is yours to arrange and pay.

Under CIF, the supplier does one thing more: it contracts and pays for carriage to the named destination port, and it buys cargo insurance on your behalf. The unit price you negotiate therefore contains a freight component you cannot see separately, plus an insurance premium calculated at the Incoterms minimum.

The part that surprises first-time importers is that CIF changes the cost split, not the risk split. Both terms hand risk to the buyer at the same physical moment. The table below is the shortest way to see where the line falls.

Term Who books the ocean freight Who pays freight and insurance Risk passes to buyer Buyer controls carrier and routing Typical fit
FOB Shenzhen Buyer or buyer's nominated forwarder Buyer pays ocean freight and insurance When goods are on board at origin port Yes, fully Buyers who want landed-cost visibility or plan to consolidate
CFR (C&F) Supplier Supplier pays freight; buyer insures When goods are on board at origin port No Buyers with their own annual cargo policy
CIF Supplier Supplier pays freight and minimum insurance When goods are on board at origin port No First shipments, single-invoice simplicity, L/C document sets
DDP (industry-typical practice) Supplier Supplier pays everything including duty At the named delivery point No Small courier-style replenishments; rarely wise for containers

Both FOB and CIF are waterway-only terms

FOB, CFR and CIF are written for sea and inland waterway transport. If your first order is 200 pieces of a heated massager travelling by air, the correct terms are FCA, CPT or CIP — quoting "FOB Shenzhen" against an air shipment creates a document set that does not match the movement of the goods, which is exactly the kind of mismatch that slows a customs entry or an insurance claim.

Under FOB, who controls the freight and what do I have to book?

You do: from the moment the container is loaded in Shenzhen, the booking, the routing, the insurance and every cost to your door are yours.

That is the whole point of FOB, and it is why LIGHT BDB quotes FOB Shenzhen as standard and works with your nominated forwarder. You are not asking permission to use your own logistics partner; the term is built around it.

Practically, an FOB buyer books and pays for:

  • Forwarder nomination and the booking itself — usually placed two to three weeks before the cargo ready date, though peak-season space can need longer.
  • Marine cargo insurance — your policy, your coverage level, your claim. Most buyers in this category insure at 110% of invoice value on all-risks terms.
  • The destination agent — terminal handling, customs broker, drayage and the final leg to your 3PL or warehouse.
  • Duty, taxes and import clearance — always the importer's obligation regardless of term.
  • Consolidation decisions — whether this shipment shares a container with your packaging supplier, your electronics supplier or nothing at all.

What the supplier still owns under FOB is narrower but not trivial: export clearance, export packing, loading the container at the plant or origin warehouse, the export documents, and getting the bill of lading into your hands promptly so you can take title and clear the goods.

Why FOB is the better learning term for a new brand

A first order tells you what your product actually costs to land. Under FOB you get that answer in separate lines — unit price, freight, insurance, duty, destination charges — and you can benchmark each one next time. Under CIF you get a single number and no way to tell whether the freight inside it was competitive. Paying for the lesson once is cheaper than paying an invisible margin on every reorder for three years.

Under CIF, where does my freight control actually stop?

At the destination port: the supplier controls booking, carrier choice and insurance until the cargo arrives, then you take over unloading, clearance and inland delivery.

Between those two points, your influence is limited to what you negotiated into the contract. In practice, CIF buyers typically cannot choose the carrier, cannot choose the routing or transhipment port, cannot set the sailing date to match a retail launch, and cannot see whether the freight they are paying is a spot rate or a marked-up one.

Three operational details matter more than they look:

  1. The bill of lading. Under CIF the supplier is the shipper. You may receive a house bill of lading from the supplier's freight forwarder rather than a carrier master bill, which adds a layer between you and the vessel and can complicate a claim or a title transfer.
  2. The insurance policy. The seller buys cover that satisfies the Incoterms minimum, not cover that suits your risk appetite. You may not be named as an assured or loss payee unless you ask.
  3. The release timing. Documents under CIF are often released against payment — normal and safe with a T/T 30/70 structure or an L/C at sight, but it means your clearance clock starts later than an FOB buyer's.

The insurance clause most CIF buyers never read

Incoterms 2020 requires CIF sellers to buy cover at 110% of the contract value on Institute Cargo Clauses (C). Clause (C) is a named-perils cover, not all-risks: it responds to major casualties such as sinking, stranding, fire and collision, but it is narrow on handling damage, stacking damage, water ingress and theft. If your cartons are riding through a monsoon-season transhipment, that gap is real. Buyers who want Institute Cargo Clauses (A) — the all-risks wording — should either request it explicitly with the cost difference priced in, or insure the shipment themselves and treat the supplier's policy as secondary.

Does CIF move the risk to my destination port?

No. Under both FOB and CIF, risk passes to you when the goods are on board at the origin port — CIF shifts cost, not risk.

This is the single most misunderstood point in the CIF-versus-FOB conversation. If the container is lost or damaged mid-ocean under a CIF shipment, you — the buyer — bear the commercial loss, and your remedy is a claim under the insurance policy the supplier bought on your behalf. That is why the quality of that policy, and whether you are named on it, matters far more than the freight rate.

A related nuance: Incoterms 2020 notes that for containerised cargo the goods are usually handed to the carrier at a terminal before they are loaded on board, which creates a short risk gap under FOB and CIF. The drafters' suggested alternative is FCA. Most private label importers in this category still use FOB because the gap is narrow and the paperwork is familiar, but if you are shipping high-value electronics, ask your forwarder how they handle that window.

Which is cheaper, CIF or FOB, and why can't I compare the two prices directly?

Neither is reliably cheaper landed — the real difference is visibility: FOB shows the freight as a separate line, CIF buries it in the unit price.

A CIF quote is not an FOB quote plus freight. It is an FOB quote plus freight plus insurance plus whatever margin the supplier builds into the bundle plus a buffer for rate movement between quotation and sailing. Comparing a CIF unit price with an FOB unit price tells you nothing until you add your own freight and insurance back in.

The practical move is to request both from any supplier you are serious about, then subtract. The gap between the two is your supplier's freight assumption. Many buyers find bundled freight runs meaningfully above what their own forwarder quotes for the same lane — industry-typical, highly variable by season and lane, and not a rule you should assume before you check.

Two structural points also change the arithmetic:

  • Volume discounts cut both ways. A supplier shipping dozens of containers a year may genuinely buy ocean freight cheaper than a first-time brand buying one LCL shipment. That is the honest case for CIF, and it is worth testing rather than dismissing.
  • Small orders distort everything. At 100–300 pieces, LCL shipments are typically billed with a minimum charge around 1 cubic metre (industry-typical), so freight per unit can be several times what it will be at 2,000 pieces. On a first small order, the term matters less than the consolidation plan.

Customs valuation follows the term — and the market

As a rule of thumb, US customs values imported goods on a transaction-value basis that generally excludes international freight and insurance, while the EU customs value is CIF-based and includes freight and insurance up to the place of import. Same goods, same freight invoice, different duty base. Confirm the treatment with your customs broker before you model landed cost, because it can move your margin by a percentage point or two on higher-duty categories.

Which term should a first-time importer use for a 100–500 pc order?

FOB Shenzhen with a forwarder you nominate, even on a first 100-piece order — the visibility is worth more than the convenience.

At that volume the freight is not where your money is; your money is in learning the true landed cost, building a forwarder relationship you will need at 5,000 pieces, and holding the bill of lading in your own name. A nominated forwarder also lets you consolidate a small wellness-device order with packaging or accessories from another supplier, which is often the difference between a viable and a painful first shipment.

What are the honest trade-offs and risks of each term?

FOB gives you control and visibility but you carry rate risk, insurance duty and the operational load; CIF removes that load but hides cost and weakens your insurance.

Neither term is a moral position. They suit different stages of a brand's maturity, and the right answer can change between your first order and your fifth.

Where FOB costs you:

  • You absorb ocean rate volatility between quotation and sailing. Rates on major lanes can swing by a factor of two or more within a single year.
  • You must actually buy insurance. Buyers who skip it are uninsured from the moment the container is loaded, and the loss is theirs.
  • You need someone who can book, chase and clear. If that person is you at 11pm, the saving is smaller than it looks.
  • If you are slow to book after the ready date, storage at origin becomes your problem.

Where CIF costs you:

  • The freight component is invisible, so you cannot benchmark it or renegotiate it line by line.
  • Coverage sits at the Incoterms minimum unless you negotiate better, and claims are harder when the policy is not in your name.
  • You inherit the supplier's carrier, routing and transit time, which is awkward when a retail window is fixed.
  • Consolidating with other suppliers is impractical, because the supplier controls the container.
