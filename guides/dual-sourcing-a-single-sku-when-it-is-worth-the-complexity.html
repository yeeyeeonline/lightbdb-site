What does dual sourcing a single SKU actually mean?

Dual sourcing a single SKU means two independent suppliers can build the same product to the same frozen spec, so no single plant failure can stop your sales.

It is not the same as multi-sourcing. Multi-sourcing spreads different SKUs across different suppliers. Dual sourcing keeps one SKU and qualifies two production lines for it — same drawings, same bill of materials, same test criteria, same packaging. Both lines must be able to build the full product, not just a component of it.

Most sourcing teams run a 70/30 or 80/20 allocation between the primary and secondary line, with the split reviewed quarterly. The point is not to play suppliers against each other on price. The point is continuity: if one line floods, burns, loses a tool, or misses a component shipment, the other line absorbs the volume before your retail commitments break.

What dual sourcing is not

  • Not a bidding contest. It is a redundancy strategy, and the second source should be qualified to the same standard as the first.
  • Not a way to split different SKUs. If supplier A makes your heated massager and supplier B makes your red light panel, that is multi-sourcing.
  • Not two factories doing different parts of one product. Both sources must be able to produce the complete SKU end to end.

When is dual sourcing worth the complexity?

Dual sourcing is worth it when the SKU is revenue-critical, when lead time is your bottleneck, or when one plant shutdown would break a retail commitment.

A common rule of thumb is to consider dual sourcing when a single SKU exceeds 20-30% of your revenue. Below that, the complexity usually outweighs the benefit. Above it, a four-week disruption in that SKU can erase a quarter of your gross margin.

Lead time is the second trigger. If your reorder cycle is shorter than the supplier's lead time, you are always one delay away from a stockout. LIGHT BDB's lead time is 4-8 weeks from deposit to FOB Shenzhen for most SKUs, which is typical for the category; if your sell-through is faster than that, a second line gives you a buffer.

Triggers that justify a second source

  • Revenue concentration: one SKU is more than 20-30% of sales.
  • Capacity risk: seasonal spikes that a single plant cannot cover.
  • Geopolitical or logistics risk: production concentrated in one country or one region.
  • Launch windows: a missed launch date costs more than the dual-source premium.
  • Component dependence: the SKU uses components that only one plant can source.

Signals that you should stay single-sourced

  • Low-volume SKUs and accessories where the 5-15% premium is a large share of margin.
  • Early-stage brands that have not yet proven demand for the SKU.
  • Commodity products with many alternative suppliers and short lead times.

What does dual sourcing cost — and what does it save?

Most brands pay a 5-15% unit-cost premium on the second source plus qualification time, and in return they buy continuity, leverage, and disaster recovery.

The premium is not the only cost. Tooling may need to be duplicated, samples and pilot runs cost money, and you will carry extra safety stock to smooth the transition between lines. The savings are less visible but larger: avoided stockouts, avoided air freight, faster recovery after a disruption, and real negotiating leverage because neither supplier owns your entire volume.

How do you qualify a second supplier without doubling your workload?

Qualify the second source with the same three gates you used for the first: certifications, samples, and a pilot run.

Gate one is the certification check. For a wellness-device ODM, the meaningful credentials are ISO 9001 for quality management, ISO 14001 for environmental management, BSCI (amfori) for social compliance, UL registered factory status, and Sony Green Partner. LIGHT BDB holds all five. These are quality and compliance certifications; they do not change the fact that wellness devices sit outside regulated health claims.

Gate two is the sample. LIGHT BDB turns samples around in 7 days, and the sample fee is refundable against a purchase order. Compare the second source's sample against your golden sample from the first source — same dimensions, same finish, same function, same packaging.

Gate three is the pilot run. Use the smallest MOQ tier that makes sense — LIGHT BDB's Lite tier is 100-499 units — and run the full production, inspection, and shipping process before you commit allocation volume.

Reusable qualification assets

  • Frozen spec and BOM: one document set for both sources.
  • Golden sample: keep a sealed reference unit at each plant.
  • Test plan and inspection checklist: identical for both lines.
  • Factory audit report: reuse the same checklist so results are comparable.

What are the honest trade-offs and risks of dual sourcing?

Dual sourcing adds real cost and complexity: split volume weakens your price leverage, qualification doubles, and IP exposure widens.

Split volume is the first trade-off. When you move 30% of a SKU to a second source, the primary line loses some economies of scale, so the blended unit cost rises. Qualification is the second: two audits, two sample cycles, two pilot runs, two compliance files. IP exposure is the third: two factories see your design, your BOM, and your cost structure, so NDAs and tooling ownership agreements matter.

Quality drift is the quiet risk. Two lines can start identical and diverge within months if you do not enforce the golden sample and audit both plants regularly. And a weak second source is worse than none — it burns your time, creates false security, and can ship inconsistent product that damages the brand you spent months building.

LIGHT BDB is honest about this: dual sourcing is not always right. For low-volume SKUs, thin margins, or brands that have not proven demand, a single strong ODM with multiple plants is the lower-complexity path. When you do need two independent companies, qualify the second source to the same standard as the first, keep one frozen spec, and review the allocation quarterly. That discipline is what makes the complexity pay for itself.
