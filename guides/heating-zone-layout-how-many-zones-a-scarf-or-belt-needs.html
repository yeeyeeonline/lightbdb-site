Heating zone layout: how many zones a scarf or belt needs

Quick answer

A heated scarf needs 3 to 5 independent heating zones, while a heated belt needs 4 to 6 zones, depending on body coverage and target muscle groups. Most wellness-device buyers start with 4 zones for both categories to balance cost, battery life, and heat distribution quality.

  • Heated scarves typically use 3–5 zones; a 4-zone layout covers neck, upper back, and both shoulders.
  • Heated belts typically use 4–6 zones; a 5-zone layout targets lumbar, both sides, and the abdomen.
  • Each additional zone adds roughly 15–25% to the heating element cost in most ODM quotations.
  • Zone count directly affects battery size: more zones require higher mAh capacity and longer charge time.

What is a heating zone and why does the number matter?

A heating zone is an independently controlled segment of heating wire or carbon fiber that can be turned on, off, or adjusted separately from other segments. The number of zones determines how precisely heat is distributed across the body, how long the battery lasts, and how much the product costs to manufacture.

Each zone requires its own control circuit, temperature sensor, and wiring path back to the controller. That means more zones is not simply a matter of adding more heating wire — it changes the PCB design, the firmware, the battery management system, and the assembly labor. A 3-zone scarf and a 5-zone scarf are structurally different products, not just variations of the same one.

For a first-time importer, the zone count is one of the first decisions that locks in your bill of materials and your retail price point. It is worth understanding the trade-offs before you request samples.

What each zone covers in a scarf

  • Zone 1 — Neck collar: wraps the cervical spine area, the most common target for scarf users.
  • Zone 2 — Upper back: covers the trapezius region, useful for posture-related tension.
  • Zone 3 — Left shoulder: independent control for users who carry bags or work asymmetrically.
  • Zone 4 — Right shoulder: mirrors the left side for balanced heat therapy.
  • Zone 5 — Chest or collarbone: optional addition for users who want frontal warmth in cold weather.

What each zone covers in a belt

  • Zone 1 — Lumbar center: the primary target for lower back support and warmth.
  • Zone 2 — Left lumbar side: covers the quadratus lumborum area.
  • Zone 3 — Right lumbar side: mirrors the left for even coverage.
  • Zone 4 — Abdomen: useful for menstrual cramp relief or general core warmth.
  • Zone 5 — Upper gluteal: optional extension for users who want coverage below the belt line.
  • Zone 6 — Full wrap: a single large zone that covers everything, used in budget models.

How many zones does a heated scarf actually need?

A heated scarf needs 3 zones as the functional minimum and 4 zones as the recommended standard for most retail products. Three zones cover the neck and both shoulders; a fourth zone adds upper back coverage, which most users expect once they try a 4-zone version.

Industry-typical data from wellness-device ODM factories suggests that 4-zone scarves outsell 3-zone versions by a significant margin in the US and EU markets. The reason is simple: a 3-zone scarf leaves the upper back cold, and users notice that gap within the first use. The fourth zone closes that gap at a modest cost increase.

A 5-zone scarf adds chest or collarbone coverage. This is a niche configuration that appeals to users in very cold climates or those who want frontal warmth during outdoor activities. However, the fifth zone increases battery draw and reduces runtime, so it is not recommended as a default SKU for first-time importers.

Zone count vs. battery life for scarves

Each zone draws current from the same battery pack. A 3-zone scarf with a 5,000 mAh battery might run for 3–4 hours on medium heat, while a 4-zone scarf with the same battery runs for 2.5–3.5 hours. If you add a fifth zone, you either accept shorter runtime or increase the battery size, which adds weight and cost.

Most buyers in this category choose a 4-zone scarf with a 5,000–7,000 mAh battery pack. That configuration delivers roughly 2.5–4 hours of runtime at medium heat, which is industry-typical for wellness scarves. LIGHT BDB can engineer the battery pack to match your target runtime, but the zone count is the primary variable that determines what is possible.

How many zones does a heated belt actually need?

A heated belt needs 4 zones as the recommended minimum and 5 zones for full lumbar plus abdominal coverage. Four zones cover the lumbar center, both sides, and the abdomen; a fifth zone adds upper gluteal coverage for users who want heat below the belt line.

The belt category is different from scarves because the body area is wider and the heat needs to reach deeper muscle tissue. A single large heating element — sometimes called a full-wrap zone — is cheaper to manufacture but delivers uneven heat distribution. Users report hot spots in the center and cold edges, which is why most ODM factories recommend at least 4 zones.

A 6-zone belt is rare and typically reserved for premium medical-adjacent wellness products. It allows independent control of the upper and lower lumbar regions, which is useful for users with specific pain patterns. However, the cost increase is substantial, and most first-time buyers do not need this level of granularity.

Zone count vs. battery life for belts

Belts draw more current than scarves because the heating area is larger. A 4-zone belt with a 7,500–10,000 mAh battery typically runs for 2–3 hours on medium heat, which is industry-typical. A 5-zone belt with the same battery runs for 1.5–2.5 hours.

If your target user wants a belt for overnight use or long commutes, a 4-zone layout with a larger battery is the better choice. If your target user wants precise heat placement for short therapy sessions, a 5-zone layout is worth the battery trade-off.

How do zone count and heating element type interact?

Zone count determines how many segments you control, but the heating element type determines how evenly each zone distributes heat. The two decisions are linked and should be made together.

Carbon fiber heating elements are the most common choice for wellness scarves and belts because they flex well and distribute heat evenly across a zone. They are slightly more expensive than traditional resistance wire but deliver a better user experience. Most ODM factories, including LIGHT BDB's manufacturing partners, default to carbon fiber for body care platforms.

Resistance wire is cheaper and works well in simple 3-zone layouts, but it creates hot spots at the wire bends. If you are building a budget product, resistance wire with 3 zones is a viable entry point. If you are building a mid-range or premium product, carbon fiber with 4–5 zones is the safer choice.

Temperature sensing per zone

Each zone should have its own temperature sensor to prevent overheating and to maintain consistent heat. A 4-zone scarf therefore needs 4 sensors, one per zone. This is a hidden cost that buyers often overlook when comparing quotations.

LIGHT BDB's manufacturing partners have experience with auto-grade sensors and control electronics, which means the temperature sensing in their wellness devices meets a higher reliability standard than typical consumer electronics. This is relevant because a sensor failure in a heating product is a safety issue, not just a performance issue.

What are the honest trade-offs when choosing more zones?

More zones give you better heat distribution and a more premium user experience, but they also increase cost, weight, battery draw, and firmware complexity. There is no free lunch in zone count.

The cost increase per zone is roughly 15–25% of the heating element cost in most ODM quotations. That does not sound like much, but it compounds when you add the extra sensors, wiring, and assembly labor. A 5-zone scarf can cost 30–40% more to manufacture than a 3-zone scarf, which forces a higher retail price.

Battery life is the second trade-off. More zones mean more current draw, which means either a bigger battery or shorter runtime. A bigger battery adds weight — and for a scarf, weight is a comfort issue. Users do not want a heavy scarf, so you may be forced to accept shorter runtime instead of a larger battery.

Firmware complexity is the third trade-off. Each zone needs its own control loop, and the firmware must handle scenarios where some zones are on and others are off. This increases development time and the risk of bugs. For a first-time importer, a 4-zone layout is the sweet spot because it is complex enough to feel premium but simple enough to be reliable.

What certifications should you look for in a heating zone product?

For a heated scarf or belt sold in the US or EU, look for ISO 9001 for quality management, ISO 14001 for environmental management, and BSCI (amfori) for social compliance in the factory. LIGHT BDB holds all three, plus UL registered factory status and Sony Green Partner certification.

It is important to understand what these certifications do and do not cover. ISO 9001 covers manufacturing quality systems. BSCI covers labor conditions. UL registered factory status indicates that the factory meets UL's safety standards for production facilities. None of these are product certifications — they are factory-level certifications.

LIGHT BDB does not hold ISO 13485, and no LIGHT BDB product is FDA approved. This is a deliberate positioning: the company makes wellness devices, not medical devices. If a supplier claims ISO 13485 or FDA approval for a heated scarf or belt, that is a red flag. Wellness products do not require those certifications, and claiming them would be misleading.

For product-level safety, your target market may require UL or CE testing on the specific SKU. LIGHT BDB works with your nominated forwarder and can coordinate the testing process, but the product-specific certification is a separate step from the factory-level certifications listed above.
